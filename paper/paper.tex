\begin{abstract}
The fastest published solver for hard Sudoku puzzles, tdoku, combines a SIMD encoding of the grid with strong propagation, and on x86 its author's benchmark shows it well ahead of the other fast solvers on hard puzzles. We ported tdoku to ARM NEON and
found that on Apple Silicon its lead over scalar solvers shrinks, but it still wins on the hardest puzzles. We then
built FastBandSolver, a scalar solver on the band representation of zhouyundong and JCZSolve (a band is a row of
three boxes; the representation stores each digit's candidates per band as a bit mask), and made three changes:
(1) a \emph{stack rule} that prunes each digit's columns within each column of three boxes, keeping only choices that fit a one-to-one pairing of bands with columns (a perfect matching), a constraint Simonis
formulated for constraint models in 2005, here applied by table lookup in a band solver; (2) branching on the
two-candidate cell with the most unsolved peers (the classic degree tie-break) instead of the first one found;
(3) a fewest-candidates fallback when no two-candidate cell exists. On an Apple M4 Max, checking uniqueness (a
solution limit of 2) as in tdoku's benchmarks, FastBandSolver is 1.07--1.62$\times$ faster than native tdoku on all
five of tdoku's standard data sets, including 1.17$\times$ on the 375 hardest forum puzzles; when only one solution is needed (a limit of
1) it is also fastest on all five, by 1.07--1.61$\times$. An ablation attributes
the gain mainly to branching: the search makes 138 guesses per puzzle on that set where JCZSolve makes 363. On x86 the result depends on the processor. Measured on seven x86 cloud machines, FastBandSolver is level with or
faster than tdoku on AMD Zen~3 (1.00--1.47$\times$), slightly slower on the hard sets on AMD Zen~4 (0.92--0.96$\times$),
and about 0.7 times tdoku's speed on Intel Ice Lake and Sapphire Rapids. Hardware counters show why: on x86, tdoku
needs 0.60--0.67 times FastBandSolver's instructions, against 1.29 times on the M4, where its 256-bit operations
become pairs of NEON instructions; on the same x86 binary, Intel cores run tdoku's code faster per cycle than AMD's,
and AVX-512 saves it another 12\% of its cycles. On many cores, running one puzzle per core, FastBandSolver also has the
highest throughput at every core count; a parallel version that splits one puzzle's search between threads solves the
hardest puzzles $2.1\times$ faster on 4 threads. We also report six approaches that did not beat tdoku, with measurements: template (pattern-overlay) search, several search-tree nodes in SIMD lanes, interleaving sibling branches, lookahead
probing, triad rules, and joining band fillings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Solving a single Sudoku is easy for a computer, but solving and \emph{proving uniqueness} of millions of hard
puzzles quickly matters to puzzle generators, to difficulty raters and to exhaustive searches such as the proof
that no 16-clue Sudoku exists~\cite{mcguire2014sixteen}. Deciding whether a partial Latin-square-like puzzle has another
solution is NP-complete~\cite{yato2003complexity}, so every fast solver is a search with propagation, and the design space is
the classic one of constraint programming~\cite{simonis2005sudoku} and SAT~\cite{lynce2006sudoku}: how much inference to do at
each node, and where to branch.

The current reference point is tdoku~\cite{dillon2019tdoku,dillon2019nerdsniped}. It encodes the grid in 128- or
256-bit SIMD registers, reasons about \emph{triads} (the three cells of a row or column within a box) in both
orientations, and in its author's benchmark it is well ahead of the other fast solvers on hard puzzles, though slower than two of them on the easiest ones (\cref{sec:related}). tdoku targets x86 SSE and AVX; to compare on ARM, we added native NEON support
to it~\cite{kelly2026tdokuarm}. On Apple Silicon the picture changes (\cref{sec:arm}): scalar solvers run much faster than on tdoku's published Intel machine, while tdoku does not, and the scalar band solver JCZSolve~\cite{jczsolve2016} already beats tdoku on the
17-clue set, but tdoku keeps its lead on the hardest puzzles.

This paper asks whether a scalar solver can beat tdoku on the hard sets too, and answers yes on the M4; on x86 the
answer depends on the processor. \Cref{sec:terms}
explains the background for readers new to the area: the parts of the grid, propagation and search, bitmasks, and
perfect matchings. Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{FastBandSolver}, an open-source C++ solver (\cref{sec:solver}) that is faster than native tdoku on all
  five of tdoku's standard data sets on an Apple M4 Max (\cref{tab:sota}).
\item A \textbf{stack rule} (\cref{sec:rules}): each digit's use of columns within a stack is a perfect matching
  of bands to columns, and pruning edges that lie on no perfect matching subsumes locked candidates. The constraint
  is Simonis's~\cite{simonis2005sudoku}; our contribution is applying it with one table lookup inside a band solver,
  only when a band's columns change. Together with the band rule, it makes box and column hidden singles redundant
  in any state that has a solution (\cref{prop:hidden}).
\item \textbf{Measurements on x86} (\cref{sec:x86}): on seven cloud machines FastBandSolver is level with or
  faster than tdoku on AMD Zen~3, slightly slower on Zen~4 and about 0.7 times its speed on recent Intel processors,
  and hardware counters and cycle measurements on ten processors separate the instruction set, the core and
  AVX-512 as causes (\cref{sec:why}).
\item \textbf{Proofs} (\cref{sec:rules,sec:proofs}) that the rules are sound and that the solver counts solutions
  exactly, terminates, and stays within its fixed array of states.
\item An \textbf{ablation}, switching each of our changes off in turn, (\cref{tab:ablation}) showing that branching, not propagation, carries most of the gain:
  picking the two-candidate cell with the most unsolved peers cuts the tree by 45\% at almost no cost, and a
  fewest-candidates fallback removes another 12\% of the time on the hardest set, even though it fires at only 1\%
  of branch nodes.
\item \textbf{Multi-core results} (\cref{sec:multicore}): higher throughput than tdoku at every number of cores on the M4, an analysis showing that about 96\% of the search trees' work (counted in band updates) could run in parallel, and ParallelBandSolver, which halves the time
  of the hardest puzzles with 4 threads, with what limits it.
\item \textbf{Negative results} with measurements (\cref{sec:negative}): complete per-digit template inference,
  running search-tree nodes in SIMD lanes, lookahead, triad rules, and band decomposition all fail to beat tdoku
  here, and we say why.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background: how fast Sudoku solvers work}
\label{sec:terms}

This section explains the ideas the rest of the paper relies on, for readers who know some programming but not the
Sudoku-solving literature. Readers who know it can skip to \cref{sec:related}.

\subsection{The puzzle and its parts}

A Sudoku grid has 81 \emph{cells} arranged in 9 \emph{rows} and 9 \emph{columns}, and divided into 9 \emph{boxes}, the
$3\times 3$ blocks outlined in \cref{fig:grid}. Rows, columns and boxes are together called \emph{units}. A puzzle
fills in some cells, the \emph{clues}, and the goal is to fill in the rest so that each digit 1--9 appears exactly
once in every unit. A well-formed puzzle has exactly one solution. The \emph{peers} of a cell are the 20 other cells
that share a row, column or box with it; a cell's digit can never appear in any of its peers.

Two larger structures matter in this paper. A \emph{band} is a horizontal strip of three boxes: rows 1--3, 4--6 or
7--9, 27 cells in all. A \emph{stack} is a vertical strip of three boxes: columns 1--3, 4--6 or 7--9. There are three
bands and three stacks, and each box is where one band and one stack cross. Within a band, the three cells where a row
crosses a box form a \emph{horizontal triad}, so each band has nine of them; within a stack, the three cells where a
column crosses a box form a \emph{vertical triad}. The term is tdoku's~\cite{dillon2019nerdsniped}. The band rule only
uses horizontal triads, so from here on a \emph{triad} without a direction is a horizontal one. When
we look inside a single band we number its rows and its boxes 0--2 and its columns 0--8.

\subsection{Candidates and propagation}

A solver keeps, for every empty cell, the set of digits that could still go there: its \emph{candidates}. At the start a
cell's candidates are the digits not already used by a clue among its peers. Solving then alternates between two
activities. \emph{Propagation} applies logical rules that remove candidates which cannot be part of any solution, and
\emph{search} makes a guess when the rules run out.

The simplest rules are the ones human solvers learn first:
\begin{itemize}
\item A \emph{naked single} is a cell with only one candidate left: that digit must go there.
\item A \emph{hidden single} is a digit that has only one possible cell left in some unit. For example, if in row 5
  the digit 7 is a candidate in only one cell, then 7 goes there, even if that cell has other candidates too. When the
  unit is a row we call it a \emph{row single}.
\item \emph{Locked candidates} use the overlap between a box and a row or column. For example, if every candidate for 3
  in the top-left box lies in column 2, then the 3 of that box must be in column 2, so 3 can be removed from column 2
  in the other two boxes of the stack.
\end{itemize}
Placing a digit removes it from all the cell's peers, which can create new singles, so rules are applied repeatedly
until nothing changes. That state is called a \emph{fixed point}. Human solvers know many more rules (pairs, X-wings,
chains and so on); fast computer solvers use only a few cheap ones and search for the rest.

\subsection{Search, guesses and uniqueness}

When propagation stops but cells are still empty, the solver picks a cell and tries its candidates one at a time.
Each try copies the current state, places the digit, and propagates again; if that leads to a cell with no candidates,
the try is a dead end and the solver \emph{backtracks} to the next candidate. This is depth-first search. Its tries
form a \emph{search tree}: each \emph{node} is a state after propagation, and each branch is a guess. We count a cell
with two candidates as one \emph{guess} (one binary decision) and a cell with $n$ candidates as $n-1$.

The benchmark used throughout this paper asks each solver to find up to two solutions (a \emph{solution limit} of 2).
Finding one solution shows the puzzle is solvable; failing to find a second one proves it is \emph{unique}. For a
well-formed puzzle that means the solver cannot stop at the first solution: it must rule out every other branch of the
tree. So the total time is roughly
\[
\text{time} \approx (\text{nodes in the search tree}) \times (\text{time per node}).
\]
Stronger rules make the tree smaller but each node slower; cheaper rules do the opposite. The choice of \emph{which}
cell to guess on also changes the size of the tree, sometimes by a large factor, at little cost per node. This
trade-off is the subject of the paper.

\subsection{Sets as bits}

Fast solvers do not store candidates as lists. They store a set of up to 32 small numbers as the bits of one 32-bit
integer, a \emph{bitmask}: bit $i$ is 1 when $i$ is in the set. Set operations then become single processor
instructions: intersection is bitwise AND ($\&$), union is OR ($\mid$), removing elements is AND with the complement
($\lnot$), and shifting the bits left or right ($\ll$, $\gg$) moves every element by the same amount at once. The
\emph{popcount} instruction counts the 1-bits, which is the size of the set. For example, the set $\{0, 2, 3\}$ is the
bitmask $1101_2 = 13$, and ANDing it with $\{2,3,4\} = 11100_2$ gives $\{2,3\} = 1100_2$ in one instruction.

We write bitmasks in binary with the bits in groups of three, because Sudoku is built from threes, and the code
does the same with C++14 binary literals such as \texttt{0b111'111'111}. For example $111_2 = 7$ is three 1-bits,
$111\,111\,111_2 = 511$ is nine, the mask of all nine columns of one row, and
$100\,001\,000\;\;000\,000\,101\;\;000\,010\,010_2$ is a 27-bit mask whose nine groups are the nine triads of a band, with a wider gap
between rows (\cref{sec:state}).

A \emph{lookup table} is a precomputed array: instead of computing a function of a small input at run time, the solver
reads the answer from memory, indexed by the input. A 9-bit input has only $2^9 = 512$ possible values, so any function
of 9 bits can be tabulated in a 512-entry array. The solvers in this paper rely heavily on such tables.

\subsection{What makes code fast on a modern processor}

A modern processor core executes several instructions per clock cycle, as long as they do not depend on each other.
The number actually achieved is the \emph{IPC} (instructions per cycle). Two things lower it in search code.
\emph{Branch mispredictions}: the processor guesses the outcome of each \texttt{if} ahead of time, and a wrong guess
discards the work done after it; search code has many data-dependent branches. \emph{Dependency chains}: an
instruction that needs the result of the previous one has to wait for it. \emph{SIMD} (single instruction, multiple
data) instructions operate on a wide register holding several small values at once, for example eight 16-bit numbers
in a 128-bit register. x86 processors offer 128-bit (SSE) and 256-bit (AVX2) SIMD; ARM processors such as Apple's
offer 128-bit SIMD called NEON. A solver whose data fits a SIMD register can process a whole box or band in a few
instructions; a \emph{scalar} solver uses ordinary 32- or 64-bit integers.

\subsection{Bipartite graphs and matchings}

A \emph{bipartite graph} has two sets of vertices, $U$ and $V$, and edges that each join a vertex of $U$ to a vertex
of $V$. A \emph{perfect matching} is a set of edges that uses every vertex exactly once. When $|U|=|V|=3$ there are
$3!=6$ ways to pair the vertices of $U$ one-to-one with those of $V$, and a perfect matching is one of these six whose
three pairs are all edges. We write such a graph as its \emph{biadjacency matrix}, the $3\times 3$ 0-1 matrix $A$ with
$A_{uv}=1$ when $u$ and $v$ are joined; a perfect matching is then a permutation matrix that fits inside $A$. An edge
that lies on no perfect matching can be deleted when every vertex must be matched, since no complete pairing uses it.
By Hall's theorem~\cite{hall1935representatives}, a perfect matching exists exactly when every set of $k$ vertices on
one side is joined to at least $k$ vertices on the other. R\'egin's filtering algorithm for \texttt{alldifferent}
constraints~\cite{regin1994filtering} deletes every such edge of a $9\times 9$ graph of cells against digits, and
constraint solvers use it for Sudoku~\cite{simonis2005sudoku}. Our rules (\cref{sec:rules}) apply the same idea to
$3\times 3$ graphs, which are small enough that every case can be precomputed in a 512-entry table.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\label{sec:background}

Sudoku has been attacked with every general-purpose search technique, and a community of hobbyist programmers,
largely on the Sudoku Players' Forum, has spent two decades making special-purpose solvers fast. This section
describes what the solvers we build on and compare with actually do, from their source code and their authors'
write-ups, and then says exactly what we took from each.

\subsection{Three general encodings}

\paragraph{Exact cover.} A Sudoku is an \emph{exact cover} problem. A \emph{choice} puts one digit in one cell, so
there are $81\times 9=729$ choices. A \emph{constraint} must be satisfied exactly once, and there are four families of
81: each of the 81 cells holds one digit; each of the 9 rows holds each of the 9 digits once, $9\times 9=81$; likewise
each column, $81$; and each box, $81$. That is $4\times 81=324$ constraints. Every choice satisfies exactly four of
them, one per family: a 5 in the top-left cell fills that cell and puts a 5 in the top row, in the left column and in
the top-left box. A solution
is a set of choices that satisfies every constraint exactly once; since each choice covers 4 of the 324, it has
$324/4=81$ choices, one per cell. Knuth's \emph{dancing links} algorithm (Algorithm X
with a linked-list representation) solves exact cover problems and discusses Sudoku at length in
\emph{The Art of Computer Programming}~\cite{knuth2022taocp4b}. kudoku~\cite{attractivechaos2011kudoku}, the fastest
exact-cover solver in tdoku's benchmark, uses this formulation without dancing links: it stores for each choice its 4
constraints and for each constraint its 9 choices, keeps a count of the remaining choices per constraint, always
branches on the constraint with the fewest remaining choices, and undoes a branch by reversing the counts rather than
copying the state. Its source describes it as a faster version of Stertenbrink's \texttt{suexco.c}. In this
formulation naked and hidden singles happen automatically (a constraint with one choice left), but locked candidates
do not.

\paragraph{SAT.} Lynce and Ouaknine~\cite{lynce2006sudoku} encoded Sudoku as a Boolean satisfiability problem, with
a variable for each (cell, digit) pair and clauses saying each cell has a digit and no unit has a digit twice, and related SAT propagation techniques to human solving techniques. The standard SAT algorithm DPLL
alternates \emph{unit propagation} (a clause with one literal left forces it) with branching.

\paragraph{Constraint programming.} Simonis~\cite{simonis2005sudoku} modelled Sudoku with \texttt{alldifferent}
constraints, one per unit, and compared how strongly different propagation methods filter them. He also added
redundant constraints for the interactions between units. One of them (his Section 5.3) is our band rule: for each
value, a bipartite matching between the three rows of a band and its three blocks, with an edge wherever the value is
still possible, removing the value from any row--block pair on no matching; he notes the same constraint for columns
and blocks, which is our stack rule, and counts 54 such constraints. He solves each with a general matching algorithm
(a maximum matching plus strongly connected components). The strongest
standard method is R\'egin's algorithm~\cite{regin1994filtering}, which models an \texttt{alldifferent} constraint as a
bipartite graph between variables and values and removes exactly the edges that lie on no matching covering every variable (a perfect matching, when there are as many values as variables). Our band and stack rules are Simonis's constraints, applied
with a precomputed 512-entry table instead of a matching algorithm, which is what makes them cheap enough for a
solver that spends tens of nanoseconds per node.

\subsection{Fast cell-based solvers}

\paragraph{Norvig.} Norvig's widely read 2006 essay~\cite{norvig2006sudoku} stores the candidates of each cell as a
string of digits in a dictionary, and uses two rules: if a cell has one candidate, remove it from its peers (naked
singles); if a unit has one place for a digit, put it there (hidden singles). It searches depth first, choosing the
cell with the fewest candidates, the \emph{minimum remaining values} heuristic of Haralick and
Elliott~\cite{haralick1980increasing}, and copies the whole state at each guess. It is written for clarity, not speed.

\paragraph{JSolve.} Linhart's JSolve~\cite{linhart2010jsolve}, which tdoku's documentation traces back to the earlier
bb\_sudoku, keeps a 9-bit candidate mask per cell and a mask of placed digits per unit. It propagates naked singles
through a queue of pending placements, finds hidden singles with bitwise ``seen once / seen more than once'' masks per
unit, and applies locked candidates to groups of three-cell strips. It guesses on the cell with the fewest candidates,
stopping its scan as soon as it finds a cell with two, and saves the board on a stack to undo a guess.

\paragraph{FSSS and fsss2.} Dobrichev's FSSS (2010) is a cell-based solver of the same kind. fsss2~\cite{dobrichev2014fsss2}
(2014) switches to a per-digit layout: for each digit, one 128-bit word whose bits 0--80 mark the cells where the
digit is possible and whose upper bits mark the units where it is not yet placed. Naked singles are found by counting,
across the nine digit words, how many digits each cell admits, using bit-parallel adders that process all 81 cells at
once; hidden singles by checking each unsolved unit for a single bit; and locked candidates and naked pairs are
compile-time options. By default it guesses on a cell with the fewest candidates, taking the lowest digit and the
leftmost cell. Its source also contains experimental alternative guess strategies, switched off by default, including
choosing by the number of direct eliminations; a comment notes one of them as slower than another alternative.

\subsection{Band solvers: zhouyundong, JCZSolve and rust\_sudoku}

In 2012 the forum user zhouyundong\_2012 posted a solver~\cite{zhouyundong2012solver} built on a different layout,
which we call the \emph{band representation}: one 32-bit word per (digit, band), whose 27 low bits mark the cells of the
band where the digit is still possible. The forum members champagne and JasonLion refined it into
JCZSolve~\cite{jczsolve2016}, whose header credits zhouyundong with ``storing bands by digit and the Update routine'',
and champagne with ``the pairing of ApplySingleOrEmptyCells and GuessBiValueInCell''. Emerentius ported it to
Rust~\cite{emerentius2018sudoku}. Because FastBandSolver starts from this design, we describe JCZSolve in detail.

\paragraph{Update.} For each (digit, band) word that changed since the last pass, JCZSolve does the following with
512-entry lookup tables. It compresses each 9-bit row into the 3 boxes the digit can still use in that row, giving a
$3\times 3$ row-by-box pattern for the band. A second table keeps only the parts of that pattern that some valid
assignment of rows to boxes can use, and returns zero, a contradiction, if there is none. This is the band rule of
\cref{sec:rules}, though the source does not describe it in terms of matchings. A third table, indexed by the columns
the digit still uses in the band, removes from the other two bands every column that is the only column left in one
of this band's boxes: locked candidates from a box into a column. Further tables detect rows that are down to one
cell, and those cells are removed from the other eight digits' words. Emerentius notes that over 80\% of the solver's
time is spent in this routine.

\paragraph{Singles and guessing.} After the updates, \texttt{ApplySingleOrEmptyCells} counts, for each band, how many
digits each cell admits, with three 27-bit words meaning ``at least one'', ``at least two'' and ``at least three''
(the same trick as fsss2's adders, on one band at a time). Cells with none are a contradiction, cells with one are
placed, and cells with exactly two are saved, so the next step, \texttt{GuessBiValueInCell}, has a list of
two-candidate cells for free. It guesses on the first of them in board order, copying the state into the next slot of
a fixed array. If there is none, \texttt{GuessFirstCell} tries every candidate of the first unsolved cell; its
comment reads ``Kind of dumb, but \_way\_ fast code''. rust\_sudoku keeps this design, removes one hard-to-predict
branch, and when there is no two-candidate cell checks up to three cells and guesses the one with the fewest
candidates.

\subsection{tdoku}

Dillon's tdoku~\cite{dillon2019tdoku,dillon2019nerdsniped} approaches Sudoku as a SAT problem and then specializes the
SAT machinery until it fits in SIMD registers. His write-up is a careful study of the design space, and we follow its
benchmark methodology.

\paragraph{From DPLL to triads.} The write-up starts from DPLL with a variable per (cell, digit), adds clauses saying
that each row, column and box contains each digit, so that hidden singles follow from unit propagation, and observes
that locked candidates are exactly the deductions this still misses. To capture them it adds new variables for the
triads, horizontal and vertical: a triad variable for digit $v$ is true when $v$ is in that triad. Rows,
columns and boxes then become ``exactly one of these three triads'' constraints, and each triad must contain exactly
three digits. All of these are cardinality constraints, applied by one rule: when the number of literals still possible
equals the number required, all of them are asserted.

\paragraph{Band configurations.} For one digit, the nine triads of a band can only be true in six patterns, one per
assignment of the band's rows to its boxes; the write-up calls these \emph{configurations}. tdoku stores, for each band
(horizontal and vertical) and each of its six configurations, the 9-bit set of digits that can still take it. The
write-up notes that configurations add no new deductions, but ``do in one step what previously might have required
multiple steps''. The six configurations of a band are the six one-to-one pairings of its rows with its boxes (\cref{sec:terms}), so tdoku's band
state is our band rule in a different form, maintained for rows and columns alike.

\paragraph{SIMD layout and propagation.} Each box is one 256-bit register viewed as a $4\times 4$ matrix of 16-bit
lanes: the $3\times 3$ part holds the candidate sets of the box's nine cells, and the extra row and column hold the box's triad variables. Propagation is message passing: a box sends eliminations to its horizontal and vertical bands, and a band
turns its remaining configurations back into allowed triads and restricts its three boxes. Every step is branch-free
SIMD code. On x86 without AVX2, and on our ARM port, each 256-bit value is a pair of 128-bit registers.

\paragraph{Branching.} tdoku picks the band, horizontal or vertical, with the fewest remaining configurations over all
digits, and in it a digit with the fewest configurations (two if possible), and branches on keeping or removing one
configuration. It copies the whole state for the first branch and reuses the original for the second.

\paragraph{Reported results.} On an Intel i5-8600k, with a solution limit of 2 as here, the write-up reports tdoku at
about 1.5 times the geometric mean speed of the other solvers on magictour and approaching twice on the hardest set,
with 113 guesses per puzzle on that set against 15 to 367 for the others, and notes that it is slower than fsss2 and
sk\_bforce2 (by GPenet, 2018) on the easiest puzzles. The write-up also reports an experiment that bears directly on
this paper: adding strongly-connected-component analysis of the implication graph cut the guesses on magictour to 3.35
per puzzle, but every variant was slower, and the author chose ``to focus on being fast''.

\paragraph{Counting guesses.} Guess counts are not comparable across solvers without care. tdoku counts one per branch
point; JCZSolve counts one per two-candidate branch but one per digit tried in its fallback; kudoku counts only
branches with more than one choice. We count one per two-candidate branch and $n-1$ for an $n$-candidate cell.

\subsection{Recent solvers}

Three open-source solvers since 2021 build on the JCZSolve design. gz\_sudoku~\cite{shines77_gzsudoku} is a C++ rewrite
of JCZSolve; its documentation reports it slightly faster than rust\_sudoku and concedes that tdoku is fastest on hard
puzzles.

RKCR7~\cite{rkcr7_2026autoresearch} (March 2026) is a Rust solver in the same family, written by an AI coding agent in
a loop of automated experiments. It reports beating tdoku on four of six of tdoku's data sets on an AMD processor with
AVX-512, for example \SI{30.4}{\micro\second} against \SI{58.7}{\micro\second} per puzzle on the hardest set. Its comparison is not like for like: its
\texttt{solve} function stops at the first solution, while tdoku was timed with tdoku's own benchmark program, which by
default also checks uniqueness (a limit of 2). The published log confirms this: it shows tdoku making 61.67 guesses per
puzzle on the 1905 11+ set, exactly tdoku's count at limit 2 on our machine, against 34.17 at limit 1. Its solver
branches on the first two-candidate cell and falls back to the fewest-candidate cell among the first unsolved cell of
each band.

fastdoku~\cite{rocketbombs2026fastdoku} (August 2026) is the closest prior work. It is a Rust solver with two
engines, a band engine in the JCZSolve style and a reimplementation of tdoku's triad engine, and it chooses between
them per puzzle. It reports 1.06--1.63 times the speed of the faster of tdoku and rust\_sudoku on all eight of tdoku's
corpora, on several x86 machines. Its benchmark measures time to the \emph{first} solution for every solver,
consistently but not comparably with our limit-2 results, and its documentation states that its lead is specific to
x86. On ARM it ports its triad engine to NEON, but compares only against rust\_sudoku, noting that tdoku cannot be
built there; its ARM measurements come from a shared cloud runner. Its band engine branches on the first
two-candidate cell. It computes the band rule exactly, from the permutation matrices of each triad pattern, and
presents this as a strengthening of JCZSolve's table, which it describes as an approximation. We checked JCZSolve's
table (\texttt{TblComplexMask}) against the perfect-matching definition on all 512 patterns and found them identical,
so JCZSolve already applies the exact band rule.

We searched GitHub, the Sudoku Players' Forum (whose archive after 2019 is incomplete), arXiv and the
constraint-programming literature in September 2026. We found no earlier solver that beats tdoku with a solution limit
of 2, and no native NEON build of tdoku itself. The ideas we combine are not new on their own: the stack rule is
Simonis's constraint and the peer-count choice is the degree tie-break. We found no fast band solver that uses either.

\subsection{Branching heuristics, lookahead and structure}

Choosing the variable with the fewest remaining values~\cite{haralick1980increasing} is the classic heuristic, and
most Sudoku solvers use a version of it; the band solvers use the cheaper ``first two-candidate cell''. Breaking ties
by the number of unassigned neighbours, the \emph{degree} heuristic, goes back to Br\'elaz's graph-colouring algorithm
(DSATUR)~\cite{brelaz1979new} and is a standard tie-break for minimum remaining values; it has been evaluated on Sudoku in generic
constraint solvers~\cite{bakker2015generic,liu2026dibs}, though not against the fast solvers discussed here. Our peer-count choice is this
tie-break applied among the two-candidate cells; what we add is its use in a fast band solver and the measurement of
its effect there. Lookahead
SAT solvers~\cite{heule2009lookahead}, and Algorithm L in Knuth's treatment of satisfiability~\cite{knuth2022taocp4b},
choose a branch by tentatively propagating candidate decisions and scoring the result, and treat a decision whose
propagation fails as a free deduction. St\aa lmarck's method~\cite{sheeran2000tutorial} goes further and keeps the
conclusions that both branches of a split share. We test lookahead in \cref{sec:negative}.

Felgenhauer and Jarvis~\cite{felgenhauer2006mathematics} counted all Sudoku grids by enumerating the fillings of the
top band and extending them, and McGuire, Tugemann and Civario~\cite{mcguire2014sixteen} proved by an exhaustive computer search that no puzzle with 16 clues has a unique solution. Yato and
Seta~\cite{yato2003complexity} showed that deciding whether a puzzle has another solution besides a given one is
NP-complete, so no fast solver can avoid search in general. Human solvers use the \emph{pattern overlay} or template
method, credited to Myth Jellies~\cite{stuart2008pom}, which tests each digit's placement against all 46{,}656
templates; we test it as a solver in \cref{sec:negative}.

\section{The band and stack rules}
\label{sec:rules}

\paragraph{The idea in plain terms.} Look at one digit, say 5, in one band. The finished grid will have exactly one 5
in each of the band's three rows and exactly one in each of its three boxes. So the three 5s pair the rows with the
boxes: each row gets its own box. Some pairings may already be impossible because 5 has been ruled out of that part of
the row. If a particular row--box combination cannot be part of \emph{any} complete pairing, then 5 cannot go in that
triad, even though nothing in the triad itself rules it out. This one idea, checked with a lookup table,
covers locked candidates between rows and boxes, and it narrows a box that has a single place left for the digit down
to a row with a single place left, which the solver then fills. The \emph{band rule} is this check. In the terms of
\cref{sec:terms}, the band's rows and boxes are the two sides of a bipartite graph, row $r$ is joined to box $k$ when
the digit can still go in their triad, the digit's three placements form a perfect matching, and every edge that lies
on no perfect matching is deleted. \Cref{fig:band} works through an example. The rule removes whole triads; it never
chooses between the cells of one.

\paragraph{The stack rule.} The \emph{stack rule} is the same check turned sideways. In a stack, the digit appears once
in each of the stack's three columns and once in each of its three boxes, and the three boxes of a stack lie in three
different bands, so the digit's three placements pair the bands one-to-one with the stack's columns. The graph has the
three bands on one side and the stack's three columns on the other, with an edge from band $b$ to column $c$ when the
digit can still go in column $c$ within band $b$, and every edge that lies on no perfect matching is deleted: the digit
is removed from that column within that band. For example, if in one stack 5 can go in columns 0 and 1 in band 0, only
in column 0 in band 1, and in any of the three columns in band 2, then band 1 must take column 0, band 0 column 1 and
band 2 column 2, and three of the six edges are deleted. The locked-column step of JCZSolve is the special case of a
band joined to a single column, and tdoku gets the same inference from its vertical triads. Both rules are the matching
constraints of Simonis~\cite{simonis2005sudoku}, Section 5.3, restricted to $3\times 3$ graphs, which is what lets one
table lookup apply them.

\paragraph{Hidden singles.} The solver detects hidden singles only in rows. \Cref{prop:hidden} in \cref{sec:proofs} shows that this loses
nothing in any state that has a solution: once propagation stops, a digit with a single place left in a box or a column also has a single place left
in that cell's row, so the row rule has already placed it.

Empirically, in a reference solver that applies every rule to every (digit, band) in each round, removing box and
column hidden singles from a solver with both rules and row singles leaves the search tree unchanged on the 1106
forum-hardest, 17-clue, magictour and kaggle sets (284.2 vs.\ 284.2 nodes per puzzle on 1106), but propagation takes
up to 6\% more rounds per node, because those singles are then found a round later (8.11 vs.\ 7.62 on 17-clue).
Dropping row singles too doubles the tree (621 nodes on 1106).

\subsection{Definitions}

\begin{definition}[candidates and solutions]
A \emph{candidate set} $C$ is a set of (cell, digit) pairs. A \emph{solution} is a map $\sigma$ from the 81 cells to the
digits that puts each digit exactly once in every row, column and box. A solution $\sigma$ is \emph{consistent with}
$C$ when $(x,\sigma(x))\in C$ for every cell $x$; $\mathrm{Sol}(C)$ is the set of such solutions. Removing a set $R$ of
pairs from $C$ is \emph{sound} when no $\sigma\in\mathrm{Sol}(C)$ uses a pair of $R$, that is, when
$\mathrm{Sol}(C\setminus R)=\mathrm{Sol}(C)$.
\end{definition}

Every rule of the solver only removes candidates, so $\mathrm{Sol}(C\setminus R)\subseteq\mathrm{Sol}(C)$ always holds, and
soundness is the reverse inclusion.

\begin{definition}[support of a $3\times 3$ matrix]
Let $A$ be a $3\times 3$ 0-1 matrix. A \emph{perfect matching} of $A$ is a permutation $\pi$ of $\{0,1,2\}$ with
$A_{i\pi(i)}=1$ for $i=0,1,2$. The \emph{support} $\mathrm{supp}(A)$ is the 0-1 matrix whose 1-entries are the entries
$(i,\pi(i))$ over all perfect matchings $\pi$ of $A$. The table \textsc{PermutationUnion} stores $\mathrm{supp}(A)$ for all
512 matrices, computed from this definition by testing the 6 permutations.
\end{definition}

For digit $d$ and band $b$ let $A^{d,b}$ be the $3\times 3$ matrix with $A^{d,b}_{rk}=1$ when $d$ has a candidate in
the triad of row $r$ and box $k$ of band $b$ (\cref{fig:band}). For digit $d$ and stack $s$, let $B^{d,s}$ be the
$3\times 3$ matrix with $B^{d,s}_{bj}=1$ when $d$ has a candidate in column $3s+j$ within band $b$.

\begin{lemma}[idempotence]
\label{lem:idempotent}
$A$ and $\mathrm{supp}(A)$ have the same perfect matchings. Hence $\mathrm{supp}(\mathrm{supp}(A))=\mathrm{supp}(A)$, and
$\mathrm{supp}(A)\neq 0$ exactly when $A$ has a perfect matching, in which case $\mathrm{supp}(A)$ has a 1 in every row
and every column.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Every perfect matching of $A$ uses only entries of $\mathrm{supp}(A)$, by definition, so it is a perfect matching of
$\mathrm{supp}(A)$. Conversely $\mathrm{supp}(A)\le A$ entrywise, so a perfect matching of $\mathrm{supp}(A)$ is one of $A$.
Equal sets of perfect matchings give equal supports. A perfect matching has one entry in each row and each column.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Only 50 supports}
\label{sec:fifty}

\textsc{PermutationUnion} has 512 entries, but only 50 different values appear in it. This subsection explains why,
and what it means for the tables built on it.

Number the 6 permutations of $\{0,1,2\}$ and identify each with its \emph{pattern}, the three entries $(i,\pi(i))$, as a
$3\times 3$ 0-1 matrix. Two facts about patterns do most of the work.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Every entry lies on exactly two permutations:} once row $i$ is paired with column $j$, the other two rows
  can be paired with the other two columns in two ways.
\item \emph{Two permutations of the same parity share no entry, and two of opposite parity share exactly one.} The
  three even permutations (the identity and the two 3-cycles) tile the $3\times 3$ grid, and so do the three odd ones
  (the three swaps). The two permutations through an entry therefore have opposite parity, so for a permutation $\pi$
  the other permutations through its three entries are exactly the three permutations of the other parity.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fifty}
The values of $\mathrm{supp}$ over all 512 matrices are exactly the unions of sets of permutation patterns, and there
are 50 of them. A union that is not all nine entries comes from exactly one set of permutations, namely the
permutations whose patterns lie inside it; the all-ones matrix comes from 15 sets.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By definition $\mathrm{supp}(A)$ is the union of the patterns that fit in $A$. Conversely, if $U$ is the union of a
set $S$ of patterns, every pattern of $S$ fits in $U$, so $\mathrm{supp}(U)\supseteq U$, and $\mathrm{supp}(U)\le U$
always; hence $\mathrm{supp}(U)=U$. So the values are exactly the unions.

Suppose the union $U$ of $S$ contains the pattern of some $\pi\notin S$. Each of $\pi$'s three entries is then covered by
the other permutation through it, so $S$ contains all three permutations of the parity opposite to $\pi$'s, and these
already cover all nine entries. So a union that is not all ones contains no pattern outside its set, and different
sets give different unions.

It remains to count the sets whose union is all ones. An entry is missed only when both permutations through it are
left out. A set of at most two patterns covers at most six entries. Three patterns cover nine exactly when they are
pairwise disjoint, that is, of one parity: 2 sets. A set of four leaves out two permutations, and misses an entry
exactly when those two share one, that is, have opposite parity; leaving out a same-parity pair ($3+3$ choices) covers
everything: 6 sets. Every set of five or six covers everything, since every entry lies on two permutations: 6 sets and
1 set. That is $2+6+6+1=15$ of the $2^6=64$ sets, so there are $64-15+1=50$ unions.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{What it means for the tables.} The index of \textsc{SingleRows} is $\mathit{kept}\mid
\mathit{boxes}\ll 9$, where $\mathit{kept}=\textsc{PermutationUnion}[\mathit{boxCandidates}]$ and $\mathit{boxes}$ is
\textsc{OneColumnBoxes}$[\mathit{columns}]$, 3 bits. Since $\mathit{kept}$ is a support and is never 0 there (that case
returns as a contradiction first), only $49\times 8=392$ of the 4{,}096 entries can ever be read. We counted the reads

entries are spread over 56 of the table's 64 cache lines of 64 bytes, so in practice almost all of its 4\,KB is kept
in cache anyway, well within a 48 to 128\,KB level-1 data cache. Packing the table to 392 entries would need a second
table to turn $\mathit{kept}$ into a number from 0 to 48, a load that the main lookup would have to wait for on the
hottest path of the solver, to save about 3.6\,KB, so we keep the full table. The tables indexed by raw candidates do

band rule, takes all 512 values on two of the data sets, and at least 488 on every set except kaggle (247) and serg\_benchmark (325).

\subsection{Soundness of the rules}

\begin{lemma}[band rule]
\label{lem:band}
Let $\sigma\in\mathrm{Sol}(C)$, $d$ a digit and $b$ a band. For each row $r$ of the band let $\kappa(r)$ be the box of
the cell of row $r$ where $\sigma$ puts $d$. Then $\kappa$ is a perfect matching of $A^{d,b}$. Consequently: removing $d$
from every cell of every triad $(r,k)$ with $\mathrm{supp}(A^{d,b})_{rk}=0$ is sound, and if $A^{d,b}$ has no perfect
matching then $\mathrm{Sol}(C)=\emptyset$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By the row constraint, $\sigma$ puts $d$ in exactly one cell $x_r$ of each row $r$ of the band, so $\kappa$ is well
defined. Every cell of the band lies in one of its rows, so $x_0,x_1,x_2$ are all the cells of the band holding $d$. By
the box constraint each of the band's three boxes holds exactly one of them, so $\kappa$ is a bijection. Since $\sigma$
is consistent with $C$, $(x_r,d)\in C$, so $A^{d,b}_{r\kappa(r)}=1$: $\kappa$ is a perfect matching, and
$(r,\kappa(r))$ is in the support. If $\sigma$ put $d$ at a cell $x$ of triad $(r,k)$, then $x=x_r$ and $k=\kappa(r)$,
so $(r,k)$ is in the support; the removed pairs are therefore unused. If $A^{d,b}$ has no perfect matching, no
$\sigma$ can exist.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[stack rule]
\label{lem:stack}
Let $\sigma\in\mathrm{Sol}(C)$, $d$ a digit and $s$ a stack. For each band $b$ let $\tau(b)$ be the $j\in\{0,1,2\}$ such that
$\sigma$ puts $d$ in column $3s+j$ within band $b$. Then $\tau$ is well defined and a perfect matching of $B^{d,s}$.
Consequently removing $d$ from column $3s+j$ within band $b$ whenever $\mathrm{supp}(B^{d,s})_{bj}=0$ is sound, and if
$B^{d,s}$ has no perfect matching then $\mathrm{Sol}(C)=\emptyset$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
This is the argument of \cref{lem:band} with the stack's bands in place of a band's rows and the stack's columns in
place of a band's boxes; only the counting step differs. The cells of band $b$ in stack $s$ form one box, so by the
box constraint $\sigma$ puts $d$ in exactly one of them, $y_b$, and $\tau(b)$ is well defined. The cells $y_0,y_1,y_2$
are then all the cells of the stack holding $d$, and by the column constraint each of the stack's three columns holds
exactly one of them, so $\tau$ is a bijection. Consistency gives $(y_b,d)\in C$, so $B^{d,s}_{b\tau(b)}=1$ and $\tau$ is
a perfect matching. The two consequences follow as in \cref{lem:band}.
\end{proof}

\section{FastBandSolver}
\label{sec:solver}

This section describes the implementation in enough detail to rebuild it; the code is a single C++20 header,
\texttt{fastbandsolver.hpp}, of about 400 lines. It solves one puzzle at a time on one thread.

\paragraph{Overview.} The solver keeps, for every digit and every band, a 27-bit mask of the cells where the digit can
still go (\cref{sec:state}). Solving a node of the search tree repeats three steps until nothing changes. (1) For each
mask that changed, apply the band rule, the stack rule and row singles (\cref{alg:band,alg:stack}); these are the
cheap, frequent steps, done with a few table lookups each. (2) Count, for each cell, how many digits it still admits,
using bitwise tricks that handle all 27 cells of a band at once; a cell with none means a dead end, a cell with one is
a naked single to place. (3) When nothing changes and cells remain open, guess: pick the two-candidate cell with the
most open cells around it, and try both digits (\cref{alg:search}). Everything else in this section is about doing
these steps with as few instructions and as few unpredictable branches as possible.

\subsection{State and bit layout}
\label{sec:state}

Following zhouyundong~\cite{zhouyundong2012solver}, candidates are stored by digit and band. For digit
$d\in\{1,\dots,9\}$ and band $b\in\{0,1,2\}$, the 32-bit word $M_{d,b}$ holds a 27-bit mask of the cells of band $b$
where $d$ can still go, with bit $9r+c$ for row $r\in\{0,1,2\}$ of the band and column $c\in\{0,\dots,8\}$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
bits 26\ldots18 & bits 17\ldots9 & bits 8\ldots0 \\
\midrule
row 2 (cols 8\ldots0) & row 1 (cols 8\ldots0) & row 0 (cols 8\ldots0) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Within each 9-bit row, bits $3k$, $3k{+}1$, $3k{+}2$ are the \emph{triad} of box $k$. We write a band mask as nine groups of three bits, one per triad,
read from row 2, box 2 on the left to row 0, box 0 on the right. For example $111\,111\,111_2 = 511$ is nine 1-bits,
the mask of one row. Two shifts, two ORs
and an AND give the band's \emph{column set} $P(M)=(M \mid M\gg 9 \mid M\gg 18)\ \&\ 111\,111\,111_2$, the columns where the digit still has a
candidate in the band (\texttt{bandColumns} in the code), and $\mathrm{cols}(x)=x \mid x\ll 9 \mid x\ll 18$ spreads a 9-bit column
set back to cells (two shifts and two ORs; \texttt{columnCells} in the code).

\paragraph{Why one word per band, not one per row.} Most of the solver's work is on a whole band at once (the band
rule, the change test, the stack rule's AND, placements and the candidate counters), and each of these is one
instruction on a 27-bit word but would be three on separate 9-bit rows.

The search state is a plain struct:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
field & type & meaning \\
\midrule
\texttt{valueToBand[9][3]} & 32-bit & $M_{d,b}$ at \texttt{valueToBand[$d-1$][$b$]}: digit first, then band \\
\texttt{checked[9][3]} & 32-bit & $M_{d,b}$ as it was the last time the band rules ran on it (0 initially) \\
\texttt{unsolved[3]} & 32-bit & cells of each band not yet given a digit \\
\texttt{openRows[9]} & 16-bit & per digit, 9 bits: rows ($3b+r$) where the digit is not yet placed \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent That is 246 bytes. A placed digit keeps its cell in $M_{d,b}$, so a solved grid is one in which every cell
has exactly one candidate. The solver owns an array of 82 states; the state for search depth $t$ is slot $t$, and a
guess copies slot $t$ into slot $t{+}1$ with one \texttt{memcpy} (a block copy of
memory). No memory is allocated during a solve.

\subsection{Lookup tables}

All tables are computed at compile time (\texttt{constexpr}) and total 8{,}548 bytes.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{8.2cm}}
\toprule
table & entries & contents \\
\midrule
\texttt{BOXES\_OF\_ROW} & 512 $\times$ 8 bit & for a 9-bit row, the boxes (3 bits) it has candidates in \\
\texttt{PERMUTATION\_UNION} & 512 $\times$ 16 bit & for a $3\times 3$ 0-1 table of candidates (bit $3i+j$), the OR of the
  permutations that fit inside it: each of the 6 permutations (3 bits, one per row and per column) whose three entries
  are all 1 is ORed in, so the result is the table's entries that lie on some perfect matching; 0 if none fits \\

\texttt{ROWS\_CELLS} & 8 $\times$ 32 bit & for a 3-bit row set, the cells of those rows \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{The band update}

\Cref{alg:band} is the inner step, run on one (digit, band) mask. It is written as a C++ template on the band index, so the compiler generates a separate copy for each band in which
the positions of the two other bands are constants. It is also forced \emph{inline}: its code is copied into the update
loop instead of being called as a function, which avoids the cost of a call.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{UpdateBand}$_b(S, d)$: band rule, stack rule and row singles for digit $d$ in band $b$}
\label{alg:band}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\mathit{bandCells} \gets S.\mathit{valueToBand}[d-1][b]$; \ $\mathit{lastChecked} \gets S.\mathit{checked}[d-1][b]$
\State $\mathit{boxCandidates} \gets \textsc{BoxesOfRow}[\mathit{bandCells} \,\&\, 111\,111\,111_2]$
  \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent}\hspace{\algorithmicindent}$\mid \textsc{BoxesOfRow}[(\mathit{bandCells}\gg 9)\,\&\,111\,111\,111_2]\ll 3$
  \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent}\hspace{\algorithmicindent}$\mid \textsc{BoxesOfRow}[\mathit{bandCells}\gg 18]\ll 6$ \Comment{the $3\times 3$ matrix $A^{d,b}$}
\State $\mathit{bandCells} \gets \mathit{bandCells} \,\&\, \textsc{PossibleCells}[\mathit{boxCandidates}]$ \Comment{band rule}
\If{$\mathit{bandCells} = 0$} \Return \textbf{contradiction} \EndIf
\State $S.\mathit{valueToBand}[d-1][b] \gets \mathit{bandCells}$; \ $S.\mathit{checked}[d-1][b] \gets \mathit{bandCells}$
\State $\mathit{columns} \gets \mathrm{bandColumns}(\mathit{bandCells})$
\If{$\mathit{columns} \ne \mathrm{bandColumns}(\mathit{lastChecked})$} \textsc{StackRule}$(S, d)$ \EndIf
\State $\mathit{singleRows} \gets \textsc{SingleRows}[\textsc{PermutationUnion}[\mathit{boxCandidates}]$
  \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent}\hspace{\algorithmicindent}$\mid \textsc{OneColumnBoxes}[\mathit{columns}]\ll 9]$
\State $\mathit{openRowsInBand} \gets (S.\mathit{openRows}[d-1] \gg 3b) \,\&\, 111_2$ \Comment{rows where $d$ is not placed yet}
  \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent}$\mathit{newSingleRows} \gets \mathit{singleRows} \,\&\, \mathit{openRowsInBand}$
\If{$\mathit{newSingleRows} \ne 0$}
  \State $\mathit{placedCells} \gets \mathit{bandCells} \,\&\, \textsc{RowsCells}[\mathit{newSingleRows}]$ \Comment{one cell per new single row}
  \State $\mathit{digitMaskNow} \gets S.\mathit{valueToBand}[d-1][b]$ \Comment{as left by the stack rule}
  \For{$\mathit{other} \gets 0$ \textbf{to} $8$} $S.\mathit{valueToBand}[\mathit{other}][b] \gets S.\mathit{valueToBand}[\mathit{other}][b] \,\&\, \lnot\mathit{placedCells}$ \EndFor
  \State $S.\mathit{valueToBand}[d-1][b] \gets \mathit{digitMaskNow}$ \Comment{give $d$ its mask back}
  \State $S.\mathit{unsolved}[b] \gets S.\mathit{unsolved}[b] \,\&\, \lnot\mathit{placedCells}$
  \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent}$S.\mathit{openRows}[d-1] \gets S.\mathit{openRows}[d-1] \,\&\, \lnot(\mathit{newSingleRows}\ll 3b)$
\EndIf
\State \Return \textbf{ok}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The names follow the code. Line 2 turns the 27-bit mask $\mathit{bandCells}$ into $\mathit{boxCandidates}$, the
$3\times 3$ matrix $A^{d,b}$ of \cref{sec:rules} (bit $3r+k$ for row $r$, box $k$), with three 512-entry lookups, one per
row. Line 3 applies the band rule: \textsc{PossibleCells}$[\mathit{boxCandidates}]$ is the set of cells of triads on
some perfect matching, so one AND removes every candidate of $d$ that no row-to-box arrangement can use. If no perfect matching
exists the table entry is 0 and the band is a contradiction (line 4). Lines 6--7 run the stack rule only when the
band's column set changed; because \texttt{checked} starts at 0, every band triggers it once on its first update. The
stack rule may shrink this band again; since \texttt{checked} keeps the value from line 5, the band then differs from
its checked value and is updated again in the next pass.

Line 8 finds the rows that are down to one cell without examining the rows bit by bit. \textsc{SingleRows} is
built from its definition: for an index made of a 9-bit $\mathit{kept}$ and a 3-bit $\mathit{boxes}$, it sets the bit
of row $r$ when row $r$ of $\mathit{kept}$ has exactly one 1, in some box $k$, and bit $k$ of $\mathit{boxes}$ is set.
The lemma says that this gives the right rows.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:singlerows}
In a call of \textsc{UpdateBand} that gets past line 4, the set $\mathit{singleRows}$ computed at line 8 is exactly the
set of rows of the band that contain exactly one cell of $\mathit{bandCells}$, the mask left by line 3.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $A$ be the matrix $\mathit{boxCandidates}$ of line 2, built from the mask before line 3. Line 8 reads
\textsc{SingleRows} with $\mathit{kept}=\textsc{PermutationUnion}[A]=\mathrm{supp}(A)$ and
$\mathit{boxes}=\textsc{OneColumnBoxes}[P(\mathit{bandCells})]$, the boxes with exactly one column in
$P(\mathit{bandCells})$ (line 6).

Line 3 keeps the cells of the triads in $\mathrm{supp}(A)$ and removes the others. Each triad in $\mathrm{supp}(A)$ has
a 1 in $A$, so it contains a cell, which line 3 keeps. So the cells of row $r$ in $\mathit{bandCells}$ lie in the boxes
$k$ with $\mathrm{supp}(A)_{rk}=1$, and each of these boxes holds at least one of them. As $\mathit{bandCells}\neq 0$
(line 4), $\mathrm{supp}(A)\neq 0$, so by \cref{lem:idempotent} every row of $\mathrm{supp}(A)$ has at least one 1.

If row $r$ of $\mathrm{supp}(A)$ has two or more 1s, row $r$ has at least two cells in $\mathit{bandCells}$, and
\textsc{SingleRows} does not set its bit. If it has exactly one, in box $k$, then every perfect matching of $A$ pairs
row $r$ with box $k$, since its entry in row $r$ is a 1 of $\mathrm{supp}(A)$. So no perfect matching uses $(r',k)$
for a row $r'\neq r$, these entries are 0 in $\mathrm{supp}(A)$, and the only cells of $\mathit{bandCells}$ in box $k$
are those of row $r$. The columns of box $k$ in $P(\mathit{bandCells})$ are therefore the columns of row $r$'s cells
there, one column per cell. Hence row $r$ has exactly one cell exactly when box $k$ has exactly one column in
$P(\mathit{bandCells})$, that is, when bit $k$ of $\mathit{boxes}$ is set, which is when \textsc{SingleRows} sets the
bit of row $r$.
\end{proof}

\noindent So \textsc{SingleRows} is indexed by the 9-bit permutation union $\mathrm{supp}(A)$ and the 3-bit set of one-column boxes, 4096
entries. Line 9 keeps only rows where $d$ is not yet placed, so each placement is made once. Placing row singles (lines 10--15) clears the placed cells from all nine digits of the band in straight-line code and
then restores $d$'s own mask, which is cheaper than testing \texttt{other != digit} in the loop. It restores the mask
as it was just before the clearing, after the stack rule, not the band-rule output $\mathit{bandCells}$: the stack rule
may have removed more of this band, and restoring $\mathit{bandCells}$ would put those candidates back (\cref{sec:pitfalls}).

\subsection{The stack rule}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{StackRule}$(S, d)$}
\label{alg:stack}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\mathit{columns}_b \gets \mathrm{bandColumns}(S.\mathit{valueToBand}[d-1][b])$ for $b = 0,1,2$ \Comment{9-bit column sets}
\State $\mathit{keptColumns}_0, \mathit{keptColumns}_1, \mathit{keptColumns}_2 \gets 0$
\For{$\mathit{shift} \gets 0, 3, 6$} \Comment{one pass per stack; $\mathit{shift}$ is its first column}
  \State $\mathit{columnCandidates} \gets (\mathit{columns}_0\gg \mathit{shift} \,\&\, 111_2) \mid (\mathit{columns}_1\gg \mathit{shift} \,\&\, 111_2)\ll 3$
  \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent}\hspace{\algorithmicindent}$\mid (\mathit{columns}_2\gg \mathit{shift} \,\&\, 111_2)\ll 6$ \Comment{the matrix $B^{d,s}$: bands $\times$ columns}
  \State $\mathit{kept} \gets \textsc{PermutationUnion}[\mathit{columnCandidates}]$
  \State $\mathit{keptColumns}_b \gets \mathit{keptColumns}_b \mid ((\mathit{kept} \gg 3b)\,\&\,111_2)\ll \mathit{shift}$ for $b = 0,1,2$
\EndFor
\State $S.\mathit{valueToBand}[d-1][b] \gets S.\mathit{valueToBand}[d-1][b] \,\&\, \mathrm{columnCells}(\mathit{keptColumns}_b)$ for $b = 0,1,2$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\Cref{alg:stack} builds, for each stack, the $3\times 3$ matrix of bands by the stack's three columns and keeps the
pairs on some perfect matching, reusing \textsc{PermutationUnion}. It is branch-free: three lookups and a few dozen shifts,
ANDs and ORs. The locked-column step of JCZSolve is the case where a band has one column
in a stack; we removed it, as the stack rule covers it and runs only when column sets change. Processing only the
stacks whose columns changed produced the same tree but was 19\% slower, because the per-stack branches mispredict.

\subsection{The update loop and propagation}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{Update}$(S)$ and \textsc{Propagate}$(S)$}
\label{alg:propagate}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Update}{$S$}
  \Repeat
    \State $\mathit{ranAnyCheck} \gets \mathbf{false}$
    \For{$d \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $9$}
      \For{$b \gets 0$ \textbf{to} $2$} \Comment{unrolled}
        \If{$S.\mathit{valueToBand}[d-1][b] \ne S.\mathit{checked}[d-1][b]$}
          \If{\textsc{UpdateBand}$_b(S,d)$ fails} \Return \textbf{contradiction} \EndIf
          \State $\mathit{ranAnyCheck} \gets \mathbf{true}$
        \EndIf
      \EndFor
    \EndFor
  \Until{\textbf{not} $\mathit{ranAnyCheck}$} \Comment{a pass ran no \textsc{UpdateBand}}
\EndFunction
\Function{Propagate}{$S$}
  \Loop
    \If{\textsc{Update}$(S)$ fails} \Return \textbf{contradiction} \EndIf
    \State $\mathit{placed} \gets \mathbf{false}$; \ $\mathit{multiple} \gets 0$
    \For{$b \gets 0$ \textbf{to} $2$}
      \State $\mathit{once}, \mathit{twice}, \mathit{thrice} \gets 0$
      \For{$d \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $9$} \Comment{bit-sliced counters, saturating at 3}
        \State $\mathit{thrice} \gets \mathit{thrice} \mid (\mathit{twice} \,\&\, M_{d,b})$; \ $\mathit{twice} \gets \mathit{twice} \mid (\mathit{once} \,\&\, M_{d,b})$
          \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent}\hspace{\algorithmicindent}\hspace{\algorithmicindent}\hspace{\algorithmicindent}$\mathit{once} \gets \mathit{once} \mid M_{d,b}$
      \EndFor
      \If{$\mathit{once} \ne 2^{27}-1$} \Return \textbf{contradiction} \Comment{a cell with no candidate} \EndIf
      \State $\mathit{pairs}_b \gets \mathit{twice} \,\&\, \lnot \mathit{thrice}$; \ $\mathit{multiple} \gets \mathit{multiple} \mid \mathit{twice}$
      \For{each cell $x$ in $\mathit{once} \,\&\, \lnot \mathit{twice} \,\&\, S.\mathit{unsolved}[b]$} \Comment{naked singles}
        \State $d \gets$ the digit with $x \in M_{d,b}$; \textbf{if} none \Return \textbf{contradiction}
        \State \textsc{ClearPeers}$(S, d, b, x)$; \ $\mathit{placed} \gets \mathbf{true}$
      \EndFor
    \EndFor
    \If{\textbf{not} $\mathit{placed}$}
      \State \Return ($\mathit{multiple} \ne 0$ ? \textbf{open} : \textbf{solved})
    \EndIf
  \EndLoop
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\textsc{Update} (\cref{alg:propagate}) scans the 27 masks in digit order and runs \textsc{UpdateBand} on each one that
changed, until a pass changes nothing. Changes made during a pass (by the stack rule, or by row singles clearing cells
of other digits) are picked up later in the same pass or in the next one; the order does not affect the fixed point,
because every rule only removes candidates, and removing a candidate never makes a later removal wrong
(the rules are \emph{monotone}), so any order of applying them reaches the same end state.

\textsc{Propagate} then counts, for each band, how many digits each cell still admits. The three words
$\mathit{once}$, $\mathit{twice}$ and $\mathit{thrice}$ are bit-sliced saturating counters: bit $x$ of each is set when at least one, two or three digits admit cell $x$, so
27 cells are counted with five operations per digit (two ANDs and three ORs, all 27 cells at once). A cell in no digit's mask is a contradiction; cells with exactly
two candidates ($\mathit{twice}\,\&\,\lnot \mathit{thrice}$) are the branching candidates; cells with one candidate that are not yet marked
solved are naked singles. A naked single of digit $d$ at cell $x$ is placed by \textsc{ClearPeers}: $M_{d,b}$ is ANDed
with \textsc{AfterPlacing}$[x]$ (removing $d$ from the rest of $x$'s row and box), the other two bands of $d$ are ANDed
with \textsc{OtherBandsAfterPlacing}$[x]$ (removing $d$ from $x$'s column), $x$ is cleared from \texttt{unsolved}, and $x$'s row
is cleared from $d$'s \texttt{openRows}. The other digits need no work, because $x$ is not in their masks. If any
naked single was placed, the loop repeats; otherwise \textsc{Propagate} returns \emph{solved} when no cell has two or
more candidates, and \emph{open} with the two-candidate cells otherwise.

\subsection{Search and branching}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{Search}$(S_t)$ at depth $t$}
\label{alg:search}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\mathit{progress} \gets \textsc{Propagate}(S_t)$
\If{$\mathit{progress} = $ \textbf{contradiction}} \Return \EndIf
\If{$\mathit{progress} = $ \textbf{solved}} \textsc{Record}$(S_t)$; \Return \EndIf
\State $x \gets \textsc{ChooseGuessCell}(S_t, \mathit{pairs})$ \Comment{$\arg\max_{x \in \mathit{pairs}} \textsc{PeerScore}(x)$}
\If{$x$ exists}
  \State $d_1 < d_2 \gets$ the two candidates of $x$ \Comment{\textsc{NextValueIn}}
  \State $S_{t+1} \gets S_t$; \ \textsc{Place}$(S_{t+1}, d_1, x)$; \ \textsc{Search}$(S_{t+1})$
  \If{$\mathit{found} < \mathit{limit}$} \textsc{Place}$(S_t, d_2, x)$; \ \textsc{Search}$(S_t)$ \EndIf
\Else
  \State $x \gets \textsc{FewestCandidatesCell}(S_t)$ \Comment{fewest candidates, highest \textsc{PeerScore} among ties}
  \For{each candidate $d$ of $x$, while $\mathit{found} < \mathit{limit}$}
    \State $S_{t+1} \gets S_t$; \ \textsc{Place}$(S_{t+1}, d, x)$; \ \textsc{Search}$(S_{t+1})$
  \EndFor
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\Cref{alg:search} is a depth-first search that stops once \emph{limit} solutions are found; with limit 2 on a puzzle
with one solution it explores the whole tree. The second child of a two-candidate branch reuses the parent's slot,
since the parent is not needed afterwards. \textsc{Place} is the full placement used for guesses and clues: it removes
the cell from all nine digits, gives it back to $d$, then calls \textsc{ClearPeers}. \textsc{Record} counts the solution
in $\mathit{found}$ and writes the first one to the caller's grid.

\textsc{ChooseGuessCell} takes the two-candidate cell with the highest \textsc{PeerScore}, the first in (band, bit)
order among ties. \textsc{PeerScore}$(x)$ for cell $x$ at bit $i$ of band $b$ counts the unsolved cells among $x$'s 20 peers:
\[
\mathrm{popcount}\bigl(U_b \,\&\, \textsc{BandPeers}[i]\bigr) + \mathrm{popcount}\bigl((U_{b'} \,\&\, \mathrm{col}_i) \mid
(U_{b''} \,\&\, \mathrm{col}_i)\ll 32\bigr),
\]
where $U$ are the \texttt{unsolved} masks, $b'$, $b''$ the other bands and $\mathrm{col}_i$ the three cells of $x$'s
column in a band; packing the two other bands into one 64-bit word makes it two popcounts. At branch time, after
propagation, the unsolved cells are exactly the cells with two or more candidates, so the score is the number of
peers still open. We count one guess per two-candidate branch and $n-1$ for an $n$-candidate fallback cell, the
number of binary decisions the branch represents.

\textsc{Load} starts from all candidates, \texttt{checked} at 0 and all rows open, and places each clue with
\textsc{Place} after checking that the clue is still a candidate; a repeated clue in a row, column or box fails that
check and the puzzle has no solution. Clues are only placed, not propagated: the first \textsc{Propagate} does that.

\subsection{Pitfalls}
\label{sec:pitfalls}

\Cref{sec:proofs} proves the solver correct. The \texttt{checked} masks make the fixed point cheap to detect but must be handled with care, and we hit four bugs
worth recording for anyone reimplementing this. (1) Returning \emph{solved} as soon as \texttt{unsolved} is empty,
before counting candidates, accepted grids in which a placement had emptied an earlier cell. (2) An update loop that
skips digits with no open rows, as JCZSolve's does, never re-checks such a digit; our first version relied on that
update to clear a placed digit's column, and recorded grids with a digit twice in a column. We now clear the column
at placement time and do not skip digits. (3) Two naked singles in one band can conflict, so the search for the
single's digit must be bounded. Bugs (1) and (2) showed only on the 1905 set, as a second ``solution'' of 5 to 7
unique puzzles; bug (3) only under AddressSanitizer (a compiler option that reports
out-of-bounds memory accesses) on puzzles with many solutions. (4) The row-single step originally restored the band's mask as the band rule had left it, putting back candidates
the stack rule had just removed, so masks could grow. No test caught it: the stack rule usually ran again and
removed them, and the solver agreed with tdoku everywhere. Writing the proofs of \cref{sec:proofs}, which rely on masks
only shrinking ((I0) of \cref{lem:invariants}), exposed it; it happened about 21 times per puzzle on the hardest set. The fix, restoring the mask as
the stack rule left it, changes no guess count and no timing beyond 0.5\%. Our correctness checks compare solution
counts at limits 1, 2 and 3 with tdoku on all seven of tdoku's data sets, 709{,}764 puzzles including 10{,}000 with
several solutions and 486{,}451 with none, compare with a naive counter on the repository's tests, and validate every
solution; \texttt{make verify} runs the comparison with tdoku.

\subsection{Work profile}

\Cref{tab:work} counts what the solver does per puzzle. On the hardest set it propagates 275 times per puzzle (136
branch nodes, each with up to two children); each propagation runs \textsc{UpdateBand} about 12 times, in 3.3 passes of the update
loop. Placements come almost entirely from row singles (1{,}920 per puzzle) rather than naked
singles (84), because the solver detects hidden singles only in rows; the matching rules turn box and column
singles into row singles (\cref{sec:rules}). The stack rule runs on 70\% of band
updates but changes something on only 40\% of its calls. Dividing the instruction count of \cref{tab:counters} by the
band updates gives about 177 instructions per band update on the hardest set, everything included.

\subsection{Engineering}

We measured each change by alternating builds and taking medians (\cref{sec:setup}). Four details each moved the time
by 3\% or more on the hard sets. Forcing \textsc{UpdateBand} to be inlined saved 9\%: clang otherwise emitted a call
for each band update. The table-driven row singles of \cref{lem:singlerows} saved 5.6\% over per-row bit arithmetic.
Not skipping digits whose rows are all placed saved 4\%. Replacing locked columns entirely by the stack rule saved 3\%.
The small helpers (\textsc{Place}, \textsc{ClearPeers}, \textsc{ChooseGuessCell}, \textsc{FewestCandidatesCell},
\textsc{NextValueIn}, \textsc{PeerScore}) are forced inline as well, so splitting the code into named functions for
readability changed the timing by less than 1\%.
Three changes that looked promising were slower and were dropped: processing only the stacks that changed (19\%
slower), batching the stack rule once per digit per pass (14\% slower, because eliminations applied immediately help
the rest of the pass), and forcing the stack rule inline (1\% slower).

\paragraph{The steps that change candidates.} Besides \textsc{Load}, four steps remove pairs from $C(S)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{band rule}, line 3 of \cref{alg:band};
\item the \emph{stack rule}, \cref{alg:stack}, called from line 7 of \cref{alg:band};
\item the \emph{row-single placement}, lines 10--15 of \cref{alg:band}: for each row $r$ in $\mathit{newSingleRows}$ it
  takes the one cell of row $r$ in $\mathit{bandCells}$ (the mask as line 3 left it), removes that cell from the masks
  of all nine digits, then gives $d$ back its mask as it was just before (line 14);
\item the \emph{naked-single placement} in \textsc{Propagate}: for a cell $x$ of band $b$ found to have one candidate,
  it finds that candidate $d$ and calls \textsc{ClearPeers}$(S,d,b,x)$, which ANDs $M_{d,b}$ with
  \textsc{AfterPlacing}$[x]$ (the band without the rest of $x$'s row and box) and $d$'s masks in the other two bands
  with \textsc{OtherBandsAfterPlacing}$[x]$ (those bands without $x$'s column).
\end{itemize}
\textsc{Place}$(S,d,x)$, used by \textsc{Load} and \textsc{Search}, removes $x$ from the masks of the eight other
digits, sets bit $x$ of $M_{d,b}$, and calls \textsc{ClearPeers}. All of these steps also clear bits of $U$ and $O$,
which does not change $C(S)$.

\begin{lemma}[every step is sound]
\label{lem:steps}
The band rule, the stack rule, the row-single placement and the naked-single placement are sound. Whenever
\textsc{Update} or \textsc{Propagate} reports a contradiction, $\mathrm{Sol}(S)=\emptyset$. Hence \textsc{Propagate}
either reports a contradiction for a state with no solutions, or returns a state with the same solutions it started
with.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix a solution $\sigma\in\mathrm{Sol}(S)$ at the moment of each step; a step is sound when $\sigma$ uses none of the
pairs it removes.

\emph{Band rule.} Line 3 keeps the cells of the triads in $\mathrm{supp}(A^{d,b})$ and removes the rest, which is sound
by \cref{lem:band}.

\emph{Stack rule.} For each stack $s$ it keeps, in each band $b$, the columns $3s+j$ with $\mathrm{supp}(B^{d,s})_{bj}=1$
and removes $d$ from the rest of the stack's columns in that band, which is sound by \cref{lem:stack}.

\emph{Row-single placement.} Let $r$ be a row in $\mathit{newSingleRows}$. By \cref{lem:singlerows}, row $r$ of band
$b$ has exactly one cell $x$ in $\mathit{bandCells}$, and that is the cell the step takes. Between line 3 and line 13
only the stack rule has run, and it only removes candidates, so at line 13 the cells of row $r$ in $M_{d,b}$ are $x$
or none. By the row constraint $\sigma$ puts $d$ in some cell of row $r$, at a candidate, so if any solution exists,
$\sigma(x)=d$, and the pairs $(x,d')$ with $d'\neq d$ that line 13 removes are unused. Line 14 restores $M_{d,b}$ to
its value before line 13, so line 13 removes no pair of $d$ itself. If the stack rule had already removed $x$ from
$M_{d,b}$, then $d$ has no candidate in row $r$, so $\mathrm{Sol}(S)=\emptyset$ and the removal is trivially sound;
$x$ is then left with no candidate at all, which the next count in \textsc{Propagate} reports.

\emph{Naked-single placement.} \textsc{Propagate} counts the candidates of the cells of band $b$ and then takes each
cell $x$ of $U_b$ that had exactly one, $d$. Placements made earlier in the same loop only remove candidates, so when
$x$ is reached its candidates are $d$ or none. If none is left the step reports a contradiction (see below).
Otherwise $\sigma(x)=d$, and since a solution does not repeat $d$ in a unit, $\sigma$ puts $d$ in no other cell of
$x$'s row, box or column. \textsc{AfterPlacing}$[x]$ removes $d$ from the rest of $x$'s row and box, and keeps $x$;
\textsc{OtherBandsAfterPlacing}$[x]$ removes $d$ from $x$'s column in the other two bands. The cells of $x$'s column
in its own band lie in $x$'s box, so every removed pair is $(y,d)$ with $y$ a peer of $x$, and unused.

\emph{Contradictions.} \textsc{Update} reports one only at line 4 of \cref{alg:band}, when line 3 leaves the mask
empty. Every triad in $\mathrm{supp}(A^{d,b})$ meets $M_{d,b}$, since its entry of $A^{d,b}$ is 1, so the mask is
empty only when $\mathrm{supp}(A^{d,b})=0$, that is, when $A^{d,b}$ has no perfect matching, and then
$\mathrm{Sol}(S)=\emptyset$ by \cref{lem:band}. \textsc{Propagate} reports one when some cell is in no mask
($\mathit{once}\neq 2^{27}-1$) or when a naked single has lost its only candidate; in both cases some cell $x$ has no
candidate, so no $\sigma$ can be consistent, since it would need $(x,\sigma(x))\in C(S)$.

\textsc{Update} and \textsc{Propagate} change $C(S)$ only through these four steps, so the last sentence follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[invariants]
\label{lem:invariants}
After \textsc{Load} has reset the state to all candidates, the following hold at every point of the solve, in every slot of the array.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(I0)] \emph{Masks only shrink:} no step adds a cell to any mask $M_{d,b}$.
\item[(I1)] A cell of band $b$ that is not in $U_b$ has at most one candidate.
\item[(I2)] Before \textsc{UpdateBand} first runs on the mask $M_{d,b}$, $K_{d,b}=0$; from then on,
  $M_{d,b}\subseteq K_{d,b}$.
\item[(I3)] If bit $3b+r$ of $O_d$ is clear, then $d$ has at most one candidate cell in row $r$ of band $b$, and that
  cell has no candidate other than $d$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A guess copies a whole state into the next slot, which keeps all four properties, so it suffices to check every step
that writes to a state.

(I0) Every write to a mask ANDs it with something (line 5 of \cref{alg:band} stores $M_{d,b}$ ANDed with a table entry), except two. \textsc{Place}$(S,d,x)$, with $x$ in band $b$, first clears $x$ from
the masks of all nine digits and then sets bit $x$ of $M_{d,b}$; this adds nothing because $x\in M_{d,b}$ whenever
\textsc{Place} is called. \textsc{Load} calls it only after checking that $x\in M_{d,b}$, and returns failure
otherwise. \textsc{Search} calls it with a digit returned by \textsc{NextValueIn} on the state in slot $t$, so with
$x\in M_{d,b}$ at that moment, and slot $t$ does not change between that call and the \textsc{Place}: a call of
\textsc{Search} on a slot writes only to that slot and calls \textsc{Search} only on that slot or the next, so
everything the first child of a two-candidate branch does is in slots $t+1$ and above; and each child of the fallback
starts from a fresh copy of slot $t$. The row-single placement sets $M_{d,b}$ at line 14 of \cref{alg:band} back to $\mathit{digitMaskNow}$, the
value it had at line 12; in between, line 13 only cleared bits, so this undoes line 13 for $d$ and adds nothing new.

(I1) The bits of $U_b$ are only ever cleared, and only by \textsc{ClearPeers} (called by \textsc{Place} and by the
naked-single placement) and by line 15 of \cref{alg:band}. When \textsc{Place}$(S,d,x)$ clears $x$, it has just
removed $x$ from the masks of the other eight digits, so $x$'s only candidate is $d$. When the naked-single placement
clears $x$, $x$'s only candidate is $d$, or it has none (\cref{lem:steps}). When line 15 clears a cell $x$ of
$\mathit{placedCells}$, line 13 has removed $x$ from all nine masks and line 14 has given $d$ its mask back, so $x$'s
only possible candidate is $d$. After that, by (I0), $x$ gains no candidate.

(I2) $K_{d,b}$ is 0 initially and is written only at line 5 of \cref{alg:band}, which sets $M_{d,b}$ and $K_{d,b}$ to
the same value. From then until the next \textsc{UpdateBand} on the same mask, $K_{d,b}$ does not change and, by (I0),
$M_{d,b}$ only shrinks, so $M_{d,b}\subseteq K_{d,b}$.

(I3) The bits of $O_d$ are only ever cleared, and only by \textsc{ClearPeers} and by line 15 of \cref{alg:band}.
\textsc{ClearPeers}$(S,d,b,x)$ clears the bit of $x$'s row. At that moment $x$ has no candidate other than $d$ (as in
(I1)), and \textsc{ClearPeers} itself ANDs $M_{d,b}$ with \textsc{AfterPlacing}$[x]$, which removes $d$ from every
other cell of $x$'s row; so $d$'s only possible candidate cell in the row is $x$. Line 15 clears the bits of the rows
in $\mathit{newSingleRows}$; for each such row, the only cell of $M_{d,b}$ in it is the placed cell $x$, or none
(\cref{lem:steps}), and lines 13--14 leave $x$ with no candidate other than $d$. After that, by (I0), the row gains no
candidate cell for $d$ and $x$ gains no candidate.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[termination and depth]
\label{lem:termination}
\textsc{Update}, \textsc{Propagate} and \textsc{Search} terminate. When \textsc{Search} is called on slot $t$, the
state there has at most $81-t$ unsolved cells (cells in $U_0$, $U_1$ or $U_2$); it writes a child to slot $t+1$ only
when $t\le 80$. So the 82 slots, numbered 0 to 81, suffice.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\textsc{UpdateBand} has no loop other than its fixed loop over nine digits, so every call of it ends.

\emph{Update.} Let $N$ be the number of masks with $M_{d,b}\neq K_{d,b}$, and compare the pairs $\Phi=(|C(S)|, N)$
lexicographically: first by $|C(S)|$, then by $N$. Each call of \textsc{UpdateBand} makes $\Phi$ strictly smaller. If
the call removes a candidate, $|C(S)|$ falls, and by (I0) no step adds one. If it removes none, no mask changes, and
the only other write is to $K_{d,b}$ of the mask it was called on: that mask had $M_{d,b}\neq K_{d,b}$ (otherwise
\textsc{Update} would not have called it) and now has $K_{d,b}=M_{d,b}$, so $N$ falls by one. As $0\le|C(S)|\le 729$ and
$0\le N\le 27$, there are finitely many calls. Each pass of \textsc{Update} makes at most 27 calls, and every pass
except the last makes at least one, so there are finitely many passes.

\emph{Propagate.} Its loop repeats only when $\mathit{placed}$ is true, that is, after at least one naked-single
placement in the pass. That placement took a cell $x$ of $U_b$ (the singles are drawn from
$\mathit{once}\,\&\,\lnot\mathit{twice}\,\&\,U_b$, and the only cells cleared from $U_b$ in between are other singles)
and \textsc{ClearPeers} cleared it from $U_b$. No step adds a cell to $U$, so the number of unsolved cells falls by at
least one per repetition, and there are at most 81 repetitions.

\emph{Search.} Write $|U|$ for the number of unsolved cells of a state. We show that every recursive call of
\textsc{Search} is made on a state with smaller $|U|$ than the state its caller started with. A call branches only
after \textsc{Propagate} returned \emph{open}, so some cell has two or more candidates ($\mathit{multiple}\neq 0$), and
by (I1) every such cell is unsolved. The branch cell $x$ is unsolved: a two-candidate cell by (I1), and a fallback cell
because \textsc{FewestCandidatesCell} looks only at the cells of $U$. Each child is the parent's state, which
\textsc{Propagate} has not made larger in $|U|$, followed by \textsc{Place} on $x$, which clears $x$ from $U$. This
holds for the children in slot $t+1$ and for the second child of a two-candidate branch, which reuses slot $t$. So
along any chain of recursive calls $|U|$ strictly falls, the chain has at most 82 calls, and since each call makes at
most nine recursive calls directly, the whole search is finite.

\emph{Slots.} The call on slot 0 starts with $|U|\le 81$. A child in slot $t+1$ starts with at most $|U|-1\le 81-(t+1)$
unsolved cells, and a child in slot $t$ with fewer than its caller, so by induction a call on slot $t$ starts with
$|U|\le 81-t$. A call writes to slot $t+1$ only when it branches, which needs an unsolved cell, so $1\le 81-t$, that
is, $t\le 80$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[the fixed point]
\label{lem:fixedpoint}
Suppose \textsc{Propagate} returns \emph{open} or \emph{solved}. Then in the returned state:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] every mask equals its checked copy, $M_{d,b}=K_{d,b}$;
\item[(b)] if $M_{d,b}\neq 0$, then $\mathrm{supp}(A^{d,b})=A^{d,b}\neq 0$: every 1 of $A^{d,b}$ lies on a perfect
  matching, and $d$ has a candidate in every row and every box of band $b$;
\item[(c)] if the three masks of $d$ are all nonzero, then $\mathrm{supp}(B^{d,s})=B^{d,s}\neq 0$ for every stack $s$:
  every 1 of $B^{d,s}$ lies on a perfect matching, and $d$ has a candidate in every column;
\item[(d)] if $d$ has exactly one candidate cell $x$ in some row, then $d$ is the only candidate of $x$;
\item[(e)] if the result is \emph{solved}, every mask is nonzero.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) \textsc{Propagate} returns right after an \textsc{Update} that succeeded and a count that placed nothing, so no
mask changed after the last pass of that \textsc{Update}. That pass ran no \textsc{UpdateBand}, so it found
$M_{d,b}=K_{d,b}$ for every mask.

\emph{The last update of a mask.} Let $M_{d,b}\neq 0$ at the end. By (a), $K_{d,b}\neq 0$, so by (I2)
\textsc{UpdateBand} has run on this mask; consider the last such call. At its line 5 it set $M_{d,b}$ and $K_{d,b}$ to
$\mathit{bandCells}$, and $K_{d,b}$ did not change afterwards. From then on $M_{d,b}\subseteq K_{d,b}$ by (I2), $M_{d,b}$
only shrinks by (I0), and $M_{d,b}=K_{d,b}$ at the end, so $M_{d,b}$ stayed equal to $\mathit{bandCells}$ from that line 5
to the end.

(b) In that last call let $A$ be the matrix of line 2, so that line 3 set $\mathit{bandCells}$ to the cells of
$M_{d,b}$ lying in triads of $\mathrm{supp}(A)$. Each such triad has a 1 in $A$, so it contains a cell of $M_{d,b}$,
which line 3 keeps; hence the triads that meet $\mathit{bandCells}$ are exactly those of $\mathrm{supp}(A)$, and the
final mask has $A^{d,b}=\mathrm{supp}(A)$. By \cref{lem:idempotent},
$\mathrm{supp}(A^{d,b})=\mathrm{supp}(\mathrm{supp}(A))=\mathrm{supp}(A)=A^{d,b}$. It is nonzero because the mask is,
so by \cref{lem:idempotent} it has a 1 in every row and every column: $d$ has a candidate in every row and every box
of the band.

(c) Fix $d$ with three nonzero masks, and one of them, $M_{d,b}$. Number the calls of \textsc{UpdateBand} on it
$1,\dots,k$, write $K_i$ for the value it gave $K_{d,b}$ at line 5, and set $K_0=0$. Call $i$ runs
\textsc{StackRule}$(d)$ at line 7 exactly when $P(K_i)\neq P(K_{i-1})$, since $\mathit{lastChecked}=K_{i-1}$ and
$\mathit{bandCells}=K_i$. As $K_k\neq 0$ and $P(K_0)=0$, some call does; let $i^*$ be the last one. Then
$P(K_{i^*})=P(K_{i^*+1})=\dots=P(K_k)$. Between line 5 of call $i$ and line 5 of call $i+1$ the mask lies between
$K_{i+1}$ and $K_i$ (by (I0) and (I2)), and after call $k$ it equals $K_k$, so from line 5 of call $i^*$ to the end the
column set $P(M_{d,b})$ never changes; and call $i^*$ runs \textsc{StackRule}$(d)$ after that line 5.

Now let $\tau$ be the last run of \textsc{StackRule}$(d)$ in the whole solve. By the previous paragraph, applied to
each of the three masks, $\tau$ comes after the moment from which each column set of $d$ stays fixed. So $\tau$ reads
the final column sets, and its matrices are the final $B^{d,s}$; and $\tau$ changes no column set, so for each stack
$s$ and band $b$ the kept columns $\mathrm{supp}(B^{d,s})$ include every column of row $b$ of $B^{d,s}$. Since
$\mathrm{supp}(B^{d,s})\le B^{d,s}$ entrywise, $\mathrm{supp}(B^{d,s})=B^{d,s}$. By (b) the band of each mask has a
candidate of $d$ in every box, and the box of band $b$ in stack $s$ lies in the stack's columns, so every row of
$B^{d,s}$ is nonzero; hence $B^{d,s}\neq 0$, and by \cref{lem:idempotent} it has a 1 in every column.

(d) Let $x$ be in row $r$ of band $b$; then $M_{d,b}\neq 0$. If bit $3b+r$ of $O_d$ is clear, (I3) gives the claim
directly. Otherwise the bit was set throughout, so in the last call of \textsc{UpdateBand} on $M_{d,b}$ row $r$ was
open. That call's $\mathit{bandCells}$ is the final mask, in which row $r$ has exactly one cell, $x$; so by
\cref{lem:singlerows} row $r$ is in $\mathit{singleRows}$ and hence in $\mathit{newSingleRows}$, and lines 13--14
removed $x$ from the masks of the other eight digits. By (I0) $x$ never regains them.

(e) For \emph{solved}, $\mathit{twice}=0$ and $\mathit{once}=2^{27}-1$ in every band, so every cell has exactly one
candidate, and since the count placed nothing, no cell of $U$ had one: $U$ is empty. We show that no digit $d$ is the
candidate of two cells $x\neq y$ of the same row $r$ of band $b$. The cell $x$ left $U$ in one of the two ways listed in
the proof of (I1). If \textsc{ClearPeers}$(S,d',b,x)$ removed it, then $x$'s only candidate was $d'$ at that moment, so
$d'=d$ by (I0), and \textsc{ClearPeers} removed $d$ from $y$ through \textsc{AfterPlacing}$[x]$. If line 15 of
\cref{alg:band} removed it for a row single of some digit $d'$, then lines 13--14 left $x$ only $d'$, so again $d'=d$,
and row $r$ of $M_{d,b}$ held only $x$ at that moment (\cref{lem:singlerows}), so $y$ had already lost $d$. Either way
$y$ lost $d$ for good by (I0), a contradiction. So the nine cells of each row hold nine different digits, every digit
has a cell in every row, and no mask is zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[correctness]
\label{thm:correct}
Let $n$ be the number of solutions that agree with the clues of the input grid. \textsc{CountSolutions} with limit
$L\ge 1$ returns $\min(L, n)$. \textsc{Solve} returns true exactly when $n\ge 1$, and then writes one of those
solutions to the output grid. Both terminate and use at most 82 states.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{What \textsc{Place} removes.} For a cell $x$ and a digit $d$, let $R(x,d)$ be the pairs $(x,e)$ with $e\neq d$
together with the pairs $(y,d)$ with $y$ a peer of $x$. \textsc{Place}$(S,d,x)$ removes from $C(S)$ exactly the pairs
of $R(x,d)$ that are present: it removes $x$ from the masks of the other eight digits, and \textsc{ClearPeers} removes
$d$ from the rest of $x$'s row and box (\textsc{AfterPlacing}$[x]$) and from $x$'s column in the other two bands
(\textsc{OtherBandsAfterPlacing}$[x]$); the cells of $x$'s column in its own band are in its box. By (I0) it adds no
pair. A solution $\sigma$ uses no pair of $R(x,d)$ exactly when $\sigma(x)=d$: if $\sigma(x)=d$ it uses $(x,d)$, which
is not in $R(x,d)$, and puts $d$ in no peer of $x$; if $\sigma(x)=e\neq d$ it uses $(x,e)\in R(x,d)$. Hence
\[
\mathrm{Sol}(\textsc{Place}(S,d,x))=\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sol}(S):\sigma(x)=d\}. \tag{$*$}
\]

\emph{Load.} \textsc{Load} starts from the state with every pair as a candidate, so $\mathrm{Sol}$ is the set of all
solutions. It takes the clues in cell order; for a clue $d$ at cell $y$, it fails if $(y,d)$ has already been removed,
and otherwise calls \textsc{Place}$(S,d,y)$. Each cell holds at most one clue, so $(y,d)$ can have been removed only
by an earlier clue $d$ at a peer of $y$. So \textsc{Load} fails exactly when two clues repeat a digit in a unit, and
then no solution agrees with the clues: $n=0$. Otherwise, by $(*)$ applied once per clue, the state $S_0$ it returns
has $\mathrm{Sol}(S_0)$ equal to the set of solutions that agree with the clues, so $|\mathrm{Sol}(S_0)|=n$.

\emph{Solved states.} Suppose \textsc{Propagate} returns \emph{solved}. Then $\mathit{once}=2^{27}-1$ and
$\mathit{twice}=0$ in every band, so every cell has exactly one candidate; let $G$ be the grid that puts each cell's
candidate in it. By \cref{lem:fixedpoint}(e) every mask is nonzero, so by \cref{lem:fixedpoint}(b) and (c) every digit
has a candidate in every row, every box and every column. A unit has nine cells, each with one candidate, and each of
the nine digits is among them, so each digit appears exactly once: $G$ is a solution. It uses only pairs of $C(S)$, so
$G\in\mathrm{Sol}(S)$; and any $\sigma\in\mathrm{Sol}(S)$ must give each cell its only candidate, so
$\mathrm{Sol}(S)=\{G\}$.

\emph{Branches.} Suppose \textsc{Propagate} returns \emph{open}. Every cell has a candidate
($\mathit{once}=2^{27}-1$), no unsolved cell has exactly one (it would have been placed as a naked single), and some
cell has two or more ($\mathit{multiple}\neq 0$), which is unsolved by (I1). \textsc{Search} picks an unsolved cell
$x$: a cell of $\mathit{pairs}$, with exactly two candidates, or else the cell \textsc{FewestCandidatesCell} returns,
with two or more. It then calls \textsc{Search} on one child per candidate $d$ of $x$, in increasing order of $d$,
each child being \textsc{Place}$(S,d,x)$; the children of the fallback, and the first child of a two-candidate branch,
are copies in slot $t+1$, and the second child of a two-candidate branch is slot $t$ itself, which is unchanged since
\textsc{Propagate} returned (proof of \cref{lem:invariants}). Every $\sigma\in\mathrm{Sol}(S)$ gives $x$ one of its
candidates, so by $(*)$ the children's solution sets partition $\mathrm{Sol}(S)$.

\emph{Counting.} We show that if \textsc{Search} is called on a state $S$ while $\mathit{found}=f<L$, it returns with
$\mathit{found}=\min(L, f+|\mathrm{Sol}(S)|)$. The recursion is finite (\cref{lem:termination}), so we may assume the
claim for every call that this one makes. By \cref{lem:steps}, \textsc{Propagate} either reports a contradiction, and
then $\mathrm{Sol}(S)=\emptyset$ and \textsc{Search} returns with $\mathit{found}=f$, or keeps $\mathrm{Sol}(S)$
unchanged. If it returns \emph{solved}, $|\mathrm{Sol}(S)|=1$ and \textsc{Record} adds one: $f+1\le L$. If it returns
\emph{open}, let the children be $S_1,\dots,S_m$ in the order they are searched, with solution sets of sizes
$n_1,\dots,n_m$ summing to $|\mathrm{Sol}(S)|$. \textsc{Search} calls the next child only while $\mathit{found}<L$
(line 8 of \cref{alg:search} for the second child of a two-candidate branch, the loop condition for the fallback). By
the claim for the children, after child $i$ either $\mathit{found}=L$ and the search stops, which is
$\min(L, f+|\mathrm{Sol}(S)|)$ because $f+n_1+\dots+n_i\ge L$; or $\mathit{found}=f+n_1+\dots+n_i<L$ and the next child
starts with fewer than $L$ found. After the last child, $\mathit{found}=\min(L, f+n_1+\dots+n_m)$.

\emph{The two entry points.} Both set $\mathit{found}=0$ and $\mathit{limit}=L$ and, if \textsc{Load} succeeds, call
\textsc{Search} on $S_0$; if it fails, $n=0$ and $\mathit{found}$ stays 0. By the claim, $\mathit{found}=\min(L,n)$ at
the end, which \textsc{CountSolutions} returns. \textsc{Solve} uses $L=1$ and returns whether $\mathit{found}>0$, that
is, whether $n\ge 1$. Its single call of \textsc{Record} writes the grid $G$ of a solved state $S$ to the output (each
cell gets the digit whose mask contains it). Every step from $S_0$ to $S$ only removes candidates, so
$G\in\mathrm{Sol}(S)\subseteq\mathrm{Sol}(S_0)$: $G$ agrees with the clues. Termination and the bound of 82 states are
\cref{lem:termination}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[box and column hidden singles are redundant]
\label{prop:hidden}
Let $S$ be a state returned by \textsc{Propagate} as \emph{open} or \emph{solved}, and $d$ a digit.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] If $d$ has exactly one candidate cell $x$ in some box, then $x$ is also $d$'s only candidate cell in its
  row, and $d$ is the only candidate of $x$.
\item[(b)] If the three masks of $d$ are nonzero and $d$ has exactly one candidate cell $x$ in some column, the same
  holds.
\end{enumerate}
So a rule that places hidden singles in boxes and columns would remove no candidate from such a state.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $x$ be in row $r$ and box $k$ of band $b$; box $k$ of the band lies in stack $k$, and $x$ is in column $3k+j$ for
some $j\in\{0,1,2\}$.

(a) Since $x$ is the only cell of box $k$ in $M_{d,b}$, the triads $(r',k)$ with $r'\neq r$ contain no cell of
$M_{d,b}$: column $k$ of $A^{d,b}$ has a single 1, in row $r$. As $M_{d,b}\neq 0$, \cref{lem:fixedpoint}(b) gives
$\mathrm{supp}(A^{d,b})=A^{d,b}\neq 0$, so $A^{d,b}$ has a perfect matching. Every perfect matching has a 1 in column $k$,
which can only be $(r,k)$, so every perfect matching pairs row $r$ with box $k$, and none uses an entry $(r,k')$ with
$k'\neq k$. These entries are therefore not in $\mathrm{supp}(A^{d,b})=A^{d,b}$: row $r$ has no candidate of $d$
outside box $k$. Inside box $k$, the only candidate of $d$ is $x$. So $x$ is $d$'s only candidate cell in row $r$, and
by \cref{lem:fixedpoint}(d) $d$ is the only candidate of $x$.

(b) Since $x$ is $d$'s only candidate cell in column $3k+j$, column $j$ of $B^{d,k}$ has a single 1, in row $b$. As the
three masks of $d$ are nonzero, \cref{lem:fixedpoint}(c) gives $\mathrm{supp}(B^{d,k})=B^{d,k}\neq 0$, and the
argument of (a), with bands in place of rows and the stack's columns in place of boxes, shows that row $b$ of
$B^{d,k}$ has no 1 other than $(b,j)$: band $b$ has candidates of $d$ in no column of stack $k$ except $3k+j$. The
cells of band $b$ in stack $k$ are those of box $k$, so every candidate of $d$ in box $k$ is in column $3k+j$, where the
only one is $x$. So $x$ is $d$'s only candidate cell in box $k$, and (a) applies.
\end{proof}

\noindent Part (b) needs the three masks of $d$ to be nonzero. A state where one is zero has no solutions, since $d$
cannot be placed in that band, so a hidden single there cannot change which solutions are found. We found no such
state among the 4{,}464{,}042 states that \textsc{Propagate} returned as open or solved on all 709{,}764 puzzles of the
seven data sets, at limit 2.

The proofs above are about the algorithm; they do not rule out coding errors. Two independent checks cover the code:
every table is built from its definition at compile time, and the solver is tested against tdoku and against a naive
counter on puzzles with zero, one and many solutions (\cref{sec:pitfalls}).

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All measurements are on an Apple M4 Max (14 cores, 36\,GB), macOS 15.6, Apple clang 17.0.0 with \texttt{-O3
-march=native}, single-threaded. We use tdoku's five standard data sets~\cite{dillon2019nerdsniped}: the 49{,}158
known 17-clue puzzles~\cite{royle17clue,champagne2022all17}, magictour's top 1465~\cite{stertenbrink2005top1465}, the forum-hardest collections (375
puzzles from ``1106''\footnote{tdoku's documentation describes this set as 376 puzzles; its data file holds 375.} and
48{,}766 from ``1905\_11+'')~\cite{forumhardest}, and 100{,}000 puzzles from the Kaggle
data set~\cite{park2016kaggle}. Following tdoku's benchmarks, every solver is called with a solution limit of 2, so it
must show uniqueness, and converting the puzzle string is part of each call. Before timing, each solver must solve
every puzzle with a valid solution and agree with the others on the number of solutions. Solvers take turns over the
whole data set, and we report the median of seven runs. tdoku is built from our ARM port~\cite{kelly2026tdokuarm}, described in \cref{sec:arm}. \texttt{make sota} in the repository reproduces \cref{tab:sota} for every solver but JCZSolve (see below), up to run-to-run variation.

\subsection{ARM and x86}
\label{sec:arm}

\paragraph{Why the processor family matters.} Almost all published Sudoku benchmarks, including tdoku's, were run on
x86 processors from Intel and AMD. Our machine is an Apple M4 Max, whose cores use the 64-bit ARM instruction set
(AArch64). The two families differ in the SIMD instructions they offer (\cref{sec:terms}). x86 has 128-bit SSE, 256-bit
AVX2 and, on some Intel processors, 512-bit AVX-512, with a rich set of byte-shuffle and bit-counting instructions.
AArch64 has 128-bit NEON, whose instructions overlap with SSE's but are not the same. Code written with x86 SIMD
\emph{intrinsics} (C functions that map one-to-one onto SIMD instructions, such as \texttt{\_mm\_shuffle\_epi8}) does
not compile on ARM at all. Plain C and C++ code, by contrast, compiles for either family, and the compiler chooses the
instructions.

\paragraph{The solvers that needed no porting.} FastBandSolver, JCZSolve, jsolve and kudoku are plain C or C++ with
no SIMD intrinsics. They use 32- or 64-bit integer operations, small lookup tables, and, in FastBandSolver's case,
\texttt{std::popcount}, which compiles to a hardware bit-count on both families. We built them unchanged with Apple
clang and \texttt{-O3 -march=native}. The same source files build unchanged on x86, where we measure them in
\cref{sec:x86}.

\paragraph{Porting tdoku.} tdoku is written against x86 intrinsics and did not build on ARM: its SIMD header includes
the x86-only \texttt{immintrin.h}. All of its SIMD code goes through two small vector classes: \texttt{Bitvec08x16},
eight 16-bit lanes in a 128-bit register, and \texttt{Bitvec16x16}, sixteen lanes in 256 bits. The only ARM build we
found translated the x86 intrinsics with the SIMDe library, and because compilers for ARM do not define x86 feature macros such as \texttt{\_\_SSE4\_1\_\_}, tdoku's
own preprocessor checks sent that build down its slowest code path, written for 2000-era SSE2. Our port~\cite{kelly2026tdokuarm}
adds a native NEON implementation of \texttt{Bitvec08x16} in which every method reproduces the behavior of tdoku's
SSSE3/SSE4 code path exactly, including details such as how a byte shuffle treats out-of-range indices and which lane
wins a tie in a minimum search. \texttt{Bitvec16x16} becomes a pair of 128-bit halves, as tdoku already does on x86
processors without AVX2. The solver itself is unchanged, and the x86 build is unaffected: the preprocessed header is
byte-identical for every x86 instruction set tdoku supports. We verified the port three ways. (1) Against real x86
tdoku: the unmodified x86 build, run on the M4 through Apple's Rosetta 2 translator, and the native ARM build return
the same solution counts, solutions and guess counts on all 199{,}807 puzzles of tdoku's test and benchmark sets. (2)
Method by method: the NEON class and the original SSE4.1 code (built on ARM through SIMDe) agree on 62.9 million
random and edge-case inputs, and the test detects each of three deliberately planted bugs. (3) Sanitizers report
no errors. \Cref{tab:port} shows what the port is worth: native NEON is 1.3 times faster than the SSE4.1 path through
SIMDe and about 3 times faster than the SSE2 path, and 1.23 times faster than the x86 build under Rosetta.

\paragraph{What the port cannot recover.} NEON registers are 128 bits wide, so on the M4 tdoku runs its 256-bit
operations as two 128-bit ones, as on x86 without AVX2; the fastest published tdoku results use AVX2 or AVX-512. Width is not the whole story,
though: on AMD's Zen~3, which runs tdoku's 256-bit vectors natively, FastBandSolver still keeps level with it
(\cref{sec:x86}).

\subsection{Why: instructions and cycles}
\label{sec:why}

Time per puzzle is instructions per puzzle divided by instructions per cycle (IPC), divided by the clock speed. The
clock differs from machine to machine and says nothing about the code, so we compare cycles. We measured them with
\texttt{bench/counters.cpp}, which counts cycles, instructions, branches and branch mispredictions around the solve
loop through Linux's \texttt{perf\_event\_open}, and with \texttt{bench/m4counters.cpp}, which counts the same events
on the M4 through Apple's \texttt{kperf}.

Most rented machines expose no hardware counters: of the types we tried, only Google Cloud's \texttt{c4} (Intel Emerald
Rapids) does. Elsewhere we derive cycles and IPC from two facts. First, the harness estimates the clock before and
after the solve loop by timing $2^{31}$ dependent register additions, each of which takes one cycle; cycles are time
times clock. (An addition of a constant would not do: Intel cores since Golden Cove fold chains of those while
renaming registers, which made our first version read about 20\,GHz.) On the \texttt{c4} the estimate (3.276\,GHz)
agrees with the cycle counter (3.26--3.27\,GHz), and on the M4 with the cycles that \texttt{/usr/bin/time -l}
reports (4.42\,GHz). Zen~3 runners on GitHub
at 3.21\,GHz and on Google Cloud at 3.46\,GHz give the same cycles per puzzle to within 3\%, and Sapphire and Emerald
Rapids, which share a core design, agree to within 1\%. Second, the same binary on the same puzzles retires the same
instructions on any x86 processor, so instructions counted on the \texttt{c4} apply to all of them (three \texttt{c4}
machines retired identical counts, 665{,}706 per puzzle for FastBandSolver). For this we build every solver a second
time with \texttt{-march=x86-64-v3} (AVX2, no AVX-512): one binary, one instruction stream, on every x86 machine.

\paragraph{Instructions depend on the instruction set, and tdoku's most of all.} tdoku retires 768 thousand
instructions per puzzle on the M4, 1.67 times its 460 thousand with AVX2 and 1.91 times its 401 thousand with
AVX-512: NEON has no 256-bit registers, so each of tdoku's 256-bit operations becomes two instructions, and some x86
operations, such as extracting a bit mask from a vector, take several NEON instructions. FastBandSolver's scalar code
goes the other way, 594 thousand instructions on ARM against 666--692 thousand on x86, as does jsolve's (1.52 million
against 1.64 million). So on x86 tdoku needs 0.60--0.67 times FastBandSolver's instructions, and on the M4 1.29 times.
This is where vector width matters.

\paragraph{On the same instructions, Intel cores favour tdoku.} With the identical AVX2 binary, tdoku runs at an IPC of
1.72--1.77 on the four Intel processors against 1.44--1.61 on the five AMD ones, while FastBandSolver runs at 1.96--2.11
on Intel against 2.15--2.32 on AMD, and jsolve, the other scalar solver, shows the same pattern. FastBandSolver's IPC is
higher than tdoku's everywhere, by 1.39--1.51 times on AMD, enough to cover tdoku's 1.50 times fewer instructions, and
by only 1.14--1.20 times on Intel, which is not. On AMD the two take about the same cycles (ratio 0.92--1.00); on Intel
tdoku takes 20--24\% fewer. We have not isolated which features of the Intel cores run tdoku's instruction stream
faster per cycle; the counters say only that they do, and by how much.

\paragraph{AVX-512 adds to tdoku's lead on Intel.} The native build removes 12\% of tdoku's cycles on all four Intel
processors and 3--10\% on Zen~4 and Zen~5, and changes FastBandSolver's by at most 7\%. On Intel the ratio falls from
0.76--0.80 to 0.68--0.71, the measured speed ratio of \cref{tab:x86}.

\paragraph{Branch mispredictions.} FastBandSolver mispredicts 6{,}600 branches per puzzle on the hardest set on the
\texttt{c4}, 3.4 times tdoku's 1{,}900, and 6{,}000 on the M4, 4.0 times tdoku's 1{,}500. The counts do not change
between builds and are about the same on ARM and x86, so they are a property of the algorithms. They come from the
propagation, not the search: sampling mispredictions on the \texttt{c4} (without precise sampling, which the virtual
machine does not offer, so each sample lands just after its branch) puts 84\% of them in the update loop of
\cref{alg:propagate} and 13\% in the search. An instrumented copy of the solver counts, per puzzle, how often the
data-dependent branches there run and which way they go. The update loop asks 22{,}540 times whether a (digit, band)
has changed since it was last checked; 15\% of the answers are yes. Each band update then asks whether the band's
columns changed, so that the stack rule must run (3{,}225 times, yes 73\%), and whether a row became a single (3{,}225
times, yes 45\%). Which bands change at each step depends on the puzzle and follows no pattern a predictor can learn:
a predictor that always guessed the likelier way would miss about 7{,}400 times per puzzle over these and the smaller
branches, close to the measured count. The test ``has this band changed?'' is also how FastBandSolver skips the 85\%
of band updates that would change nothing, and so a source of its low instruction count; tdoku instead runs its vector
operations over the grid without asking, and pays in instructions. The mispredictions are the price of the skipping,
and they cannot simply be removed (\cref{sec:negative}). On x86 FastBandSolver's IPC is higher than tdoku's
despite them. Memory plays no part: every solver misses the L1 data cache fewer than 6 times per puzzle, on both
machines, against about 300 thousand cycles.

\paragraph{The M4.} On the M4 FastBandSolver needs fewer instructions than tdoku, 0.77 times as many, at 0.91 times its
IPC, and so fewer cycles: 254 thousand against 299 thousand, a ratio of 1.18, which is its measured lead. It is also the
machine on which FastBandSolver takes the fewest cycles of all, 12--27\% fewer than its native build on any x86
processor, while tdoku takes more cycles there than its native build on every x86 processor except Zen~3.

\section{Using more than one core}
\label{sec:multicore}

Everything so far runs on one core, as tdoku's benchmark does. There are two different ways to use more. The first is
\emph{throughput}: solving many puzzles at once, each core taking its own share. That is the natural setting for
rating, generating or searching millions of puzzles, and it is fair to every solver, because every solver can be run
that way unchanged. The second is \emph{latency}: solving one puzzle faster by splitting its search tree between cores.
Only a solver built for it can do that, so a comparison with single-threaded solvers is no longer like for like, and we
report it separately.

\subsection{The search tree allows a lot of parallelism}

With a limit of 2 on a puzzle with one solution, the whole tree must be explored, and its branches are independent.
Before writing any parallel code we measured how much this allows. We recorded each puzzle's search tree, with the work
of each node (band updates, the dominant cost), and computed two bounds.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{critical path}, the most work along any single path from the root to a leaf, cannot be split: it limits
  the speedup however many cores are used. On the hardest set it is only 3.5\% of the total work (5.5\% on the second
  forum set), so with ideal splitting 14 cores could give $13.98\times$ ($13.6\times$). The trees are almost entirely
  parallel work. On magictour, whose trees are small, the ceiling is $3.5\times$.
\item Splitting at a fixed depth, handing each subtree below it to a core, does much worse, at best $4.5\times$ on the
  hardest set and $3.7\times$ on the second forum set, because the subtrees are very unequal: good use of many cores
  needs branches to be handed out as the search goes.
\end{itemize}

The obstacle is size. A node takes about \SI{200}{\nano\second} on average, so moving work between cores has to cost less than that,
or be limited to branches large enough to be worth it.

\subsection{ParallelBandSolver}

\texttt{parallelbandsolver.hpp} runs FastBandSolver's search on several threads, with the same interface. The calling
thread searches as before. At a two-candidate branch it may put the second branch, a copy of the 246-byte state, on a
shared queue instead of exploring it later itself; idle worker threads take branches from the queue and search them the
same way, handing out branches in turn. A shared counter stops every thread once the limit is reached. Four details,
each found by measurement on a prototype, make it faster than one thread rather than slower:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No sleeping on the queue.} With an ordinary mutex, every idle thread tried to take a new branch at once, and
  under that contention the operating system put threads to sleep, which costs more than a whole puzzle: with 8 threads
  the hardest set took \SI{177}{\micro\second} per puzzle instead of \SI{56}{\micro\second}. A try-lock, where a thread that finds the queue busy keeps
  spinning, together with the depth limit below, brought 4 threads to \SI{25}{\micro\second}.
\item \textbf{Only near the top.} Branches are handed out only while fewer than 8 guesses deep, where subtrees are large.
\item \textbf{Only for hard puzzles.} A puzzle is searched by the calling thread alone until it has taken 16 nodes, so
  easy puzzles never involve the other threads.
\item \textbf{Workers sleep between hard puzzles.} Spinning idle threads lowered the clock speed of the working one:
  kaggle puzzles, which never share a branch, ran up to 12\% slower. Workers now spin only while a puzzle is being shared,
  and sleep after a short grace period.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Correctness.} The parallel search explores the same tree as FastBandSolver, divided between threads: until the limit is reached,
each branch is searched exactly once, by whichever thread holds it; after that, branches still waiting return without
work, as the serial search stops early too. The proof of \cref{thm:correct} therefore carries over, with two changes. The shared counter can overshoot the limit when two threads find solutions at the same time, so the
count returned is the minimum of the counter and the limit. The first solution is copied out by the thread whose increment takes the counter from 0 to 1. Each thread has its own
array of 82 states, and a branch taken from the queue starts at the bottom of it with no more unsolved cells than the
state it was copied from, so \cref{lem:termination}'s bound holds per thread. The guess count is summed over the
branches actually explored, so once the limit is reached it can be lower than FastBandSolver's. We checked the solution counts at limits 1, 2 and 3, and every solution, on
all seven of tdoku's data sets with 2, 4 and 8 threads; again with sharing forced from the first node, which makes the
puzzles with several solutions share branches; and ran the repository's tests, including ThreadSanitizer, which reported
no data races.

\section{What did not work}
\label{sec:negative}

We tried six other directions before the design above. Each was measured on the hardest set against tdoku's
\SI{75}{\micro\second} and 110 guesses; we report them because each is a natural idea and none paid off here.

\paragraph{Template search.} The template or pattern-overlay method~\cite{stuart2008pom} treats each digit's placements as one
of 46{,}656 templates.\footnote{Count them band by band. In the first band, choose which box each row uses (6 ways)
and a column within each box ($3^3$ ways): 162. In the second band, again 6 row-to-box pairings, but in each box the
column must differ from the first band's column in that stack: $6\cdot 2^3=48$. In the third band the columns are then
forced, leaving the 6 pairings. $162\cdot 48\cdot 6 = 46{,}656 = 6^6$.} complete per-digit inference means keeping only templates consistent with the candidates. After
band propagation, a hard puzzle has a median of 491 templates over all nine digits (90th percentile 787), few enough
to filter as 128-bit masks. But even removing every template that conflicts with the
candidates (generalized arc consistency), adding a rule that every cell must be covered by some digit's remaining
templates, and branching on cells, still makes 98 decisions per puzzle, and adding pairwise digit consistency gives 81: little
better than tdoku's 110, at a much higher cost per node. The forum-hardest puzzles are selected to resist single-digit techniques, and complete single-digit inference does not
collapse their trees. Eppstein~\cite{eppstein2012single} solves these single-digit subproblems asymptotically faster
than the pattern overlay method and shows that single-digit deduction is NP-hard in general.

\paragraph{Search-tree nodes in SIMD lanes.} With a limit of 2 on a puzzle with one solution, the whole tree must be
searched, so the order of nodes does not matter and several nodes can be propagated at once. We wrote a branch-free
propagation round that runs one board per 32-bit lane of NEON registers, and verified it inside a one-board search
against tdoku on all five sets; we did not build the scheduler that keeps the lanes full. One round costs \SI{74}{\nano\second} per
board with 4 lanes (3{,}344 instructions per round for 4 boards, IPC 2.8), \SI{62}{\nano\second} with 8 and \SI{50}{\nano\second} with 16, and a
node needs 3.9 rounds to reach a fixed point; capping the rounds grows the tree faster than it saves work (1{,}340
board-rounds per puzzle at two rounds per node vs.\ 1{,}119 at the fixed point). That projects to \SIrange{2.4e-7}{2.9e-7}{\second} per
node with 4--8 lanes, above the roughly \SI{220}{\nano\second} per node of JCZSolve's incremental engine under the same peer-count
branching, and \SI{195}{\nano\second} with 16 lanes, near parity before any scheduling cost. We stopped there: a frontier of 16 nodes
cannot be kept full over most of a tree of a few hundred nodes. Two things are missing: NEON has no gather, so the $3\times 3$ matching takes about 40 bit
operations instead of one lookup in a 512-entry table, and full rounds recompute all 27 (digit, band) masks where an
incremental engine touches only the changed ones.

\paragraph{Interleaving sibling branches on one core.} FastBandSolver runs at about 2.4 instructions per cycle, well
below what the core can issue, mostly because of hard-to-predict branches. We tried propagating both children of each
two-way guess in one interleaved loop, so that the processor could overlap two independent streams of work, before
searching them in turn: the same work in a different order. It was about 3\% slower on the hard sets, with identical
results; the extra bookkeeping cost more than the overlap gained.

\paragraph{Lookahead.} Probing both children of the best-scored two-candidate cells before branching, and taking a
failed probe as a free deduction, shrinks the tree fourfold: with JCZSolve's engine and 2 probed cells per node the
guesses fall from 199 to 49 on the hardest set. The time rises from \SI{87}{\micro\second} to \SI{102}{\micro\second}, and probing only near the root, at
any threshold we tried, was never faster than not probing. In a slower reference solver the tree fell further (18
guesses with 64 probed cells), but at 1{,}282 probes per puzzle. This probing is what Simonis calls
\emph{shaving}~\cite{simonis2005sudoku}, which he found a strong filter in constraint models; our result is that in a
fast band solver it costs more than it saves.

\paragraph{Richer rules and branching.} Triad rules for horizontal and vertical triads (a triad whose cells admit exactly
three digits must hold all three, and three digits forced into a triad exclude the others) reduced the tree by
6--10\%, not enough to pay for themselves. Scoring bivalue cells and bilocations (a digit with two places left in a
unit) by the candidates they touch reduced the tree by another 18\% in the reference solver (121 vs.\ 148 guesses),
but computing the score cost about \SI{900}{\nano\second} per branch node in a band solver, more than it saved. Scoring by the peer
candidates of the cell's two digits saves 1.5\% of guesses and costs 17\% of time. Branching on band configurations
as tdoku does was worse than peer-count branching with our rules (169 vs.\ 142 guesses).

\paragraph{Band decomposition.} A solution is three band fillings whose columns use disjoint digits, so solutions can
be counted by joining lists of band fillings, as in the enumeration of grids~\cite{felgenhauer2006mathematics}. After
propagation, however, the band with the fewest fillings still has a median of 888 of them on the hardest set (2{,}054
for the middle band), so the join alone is millions of pairs.

\paragraph{Fewer mispredicted branches.} After the design above, we tried to remove the branches behind its
mispredictions (\cref{sec:why}), each change timed against the unchanged solver in 7 alternating runs per data set on
the M4, with identical guess counts and, where the change touched the propagation, the full check of
\cref{sec:pitfalls}. Placing row singles without first testing whether any row is new (the step then does nothing when
none is): 0.998--1.005 times the original time over the five sets. Running the stack rule on every band update instead
of only when the columns change: 0.997--1.003. Collecting the changed (digit, band) pairs into a bit mask once per pass
and visiting only those, instead of testing all 27: 0.999--1.003. Choosing the best two-candidate cell without a
branch: 0.999--1.004. Each removes mispredictions but adds back as much, in work that the branch used to skip or in new
branches; the code is at a balance point. Profile-guided optimization, which lets the compiler lay out branches from a
recorded run, changed the hard sets by $+$0.2--0.5\% and saved 1--2\% on 17-clue and kaggle, too little to be
worth a compiler-specific build.

\section{Discussion}

The result depends on the machine (\cref{tab:x86}), and the counters of \cref{sec:why} say why. FastBandSolver
always makes more guesses than tdoku, and on x86 always runs at a higher IPC; which one wins depends on how many
instructions each needs and how fast the core runs each kind of code. On the M4, tdoku's 256-bit operations become
pairs of 128-bit NEON instructions, so it needs 1.29 times FastBandSolver's instructions, and FastBandSolver wins. On
x86 tdoku needs only 0.60--0.67 times FastBandSolver's instructions; AMD's cores run FastBandSolver's code about 1.4--1.5
times as fast per cycle as tdoku's, which makes the two level, while Intel's run tdoku's code relatively faster, and
AVX-512 cuts its instructions further. All three solvers in \cref{tab:counters} run at an IPC between 2.1 and 2.6 on
the hardest set on the M4, and their times follow their instruction counts and tree sizes: there, cheap choices of where to branch pay
off more than stronger propagation.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The M4 measurements are from one machine and one compiler. The x86 measurements are from
rented virtual machines, and we measured neither throughput nor ParallelBandSolver there. Hardware counters were
available on one x86 machine type; elsewhere cycles come from time and a measured clock, validated where both were
available (\cref{sec:why}). They include no x86 desktop or laptop processor, and we did not isolate which features
of the Intel cores run tdoku's code faster per cycle. The data sets are those of tdoku's benchmark, and the
forum-hardest sets are selected for difficulty under human techniques, not under search. Guess counts are not directly
comparable across solvers that branch on different objects, which is why we compare times.

\section{Conclusion}

A scalar band solver with a matching-based stack rule, peer-count branching and a fewest-candidates fallback is faster
than tdoku on all of tdoku's standard data sets on Apple Silicon, by 1.07--1.62$\times$ when checking uniqueness and by
1.07--1.61$\times$ when finding one solution. Branching carries most of the gain. The advantage depends on the
processor: on AMD's Zen~3 FastBandSolver is level with tdoku on the hard sets and faster elsewhere, on Zen~4 it is
slightly slower on the hard sets, and on recent Intel processors it runs at about 0.7 times tdoku's speed, as a
projection from tdoku's published results predicted. Counting instructions and cycles explains the pattern: tdoku's
vector code needs far fewer instructions on x86 than on ARM, Intel's cores run it faster per cycle than AMD's, and
AVX-512 adds 12\% on Intel. On many cores it keeps the highest throughput on the M4, and splitting one puzzle's search between 4
threads halves the time of the hardest puzzles, far below what the trees would allow, because moving work between
cores costs about as much as the work. The code and the benchmark are in the repository, \texttt{make sota}
reproduces \cref{tab:sota}, and \texttt{bench/results} holds the x86 runs.
